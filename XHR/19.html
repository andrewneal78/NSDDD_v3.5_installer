[753] Strategic Tasks for the ADF Defending Australia

[754] The Government has reaffirmed that the primary priority for the ADF is to maintain the capability to defend Australian territory from any credible attack, without relying on help from the combat forces of any other country

[755] An attack on Australia would be the most serious and demanding military contingency we could face

[756] We would want a range of options to respond effectively

[757] This provides a clear basis for our defence planning, which is clearly understood both by Australians and our neighbours

[758] And we are confident that forces built primarily to defend Australia will be able to undertake a range of operations to promote our wider strategic objectives

[759] The Government's approach to this task is shaped by the following principles

[760] Self-Reliance. Our armed forces need to be able to defend Australia without relying on the combat forces of other countries

[761] This principle of self-reliance reflects, fundamentally, our sense of ourselves as a nation

[762] As we made clear in discussing our US alliance in Chapter Five, the Government's commitment to self-reliance does not reflect any lack of confidence in our allies

[763] Nor does it suggest that we would not seek and expect help from our allies and friends in time of need

[764] It simply means that we should not rely on others having either the capacity or the willingness to defend our country, especially if we have not taken the effort to provide effectively for our own defence

[765] Moreover, self-reliance does not preclude us from planning on a significant degree of support in non-combat areas including intelligence and surveillance, resupply and logistics

[766] A Maritime Strategy

[767] The key to defending Australia is to control the air and sea approaches to our continent, so as to deny them to hostile ships and aircraft, and provide maximum freedom of action for our forces

[768] That means we need a fundamentally maritime strategy

[769] Our strategic geography, our relatively small population and our comparative advantage in a range of technologies all dictate that our defence should focus on our air and sea approaches

[770] The nature of our air and sea approaches is such that a maritime strategy includes a vital and central role for land forces

[771] They would assist air and naval forces to control those approaches and would be needed to defeat any incursions onto our territory

[772] A key role would be to ensure the security of the bases from which our air and naval forces operate

[773] They provide the foundations for a maritime defence posture

[774] The scale of our land forces also dictates the strength of forces needed for any hostile lodgment in our approaches or on Australian soil

[775] The larger those forces need to be, the more support they would need - and the more vulnerable they would be to our air and naval forces

[776] Australia’s strategic posture is defensive in the most fundamental sense

[777] We would not initiate the threat or the use of force, and our objectives in conflict would be to terminate hostile operations against us as quickly as possible, and to our maximum benefit, at minimum cost in lives and resources

[778] But that does not mean that our approach would be operationally defensive

[779] On the contrary, if attacked, Australia would take a highly proactive approach in order to secure a rapid and favourable end to hostilities

[780] We would be concerned to ensure that hostilities were concluded swiftly and decisively, without harming unnecessarily the prospects for future relations with the adversary

[781] However, we would aim to minimise Australian casualties and damage

[782] We would therefore seek to attack hostile forces as far from our shores as possible, including in their home bases, forward operating bases and in transit

[783] We would aim to seize the initiative and dictate the pace, location and intensity of operations

[785] Australia needs to be able to work with our neighbours to respond in the very unlikely event of armed aggression against them

[786] We also need to be able to join UN-sanctioned international operations to help them manage other challenges to their stability

[787] This might require the ADF to contribute to regional peacekeeping and humanitarian relief operations and help evacuate Australians and others from regional troublespots

[788] We should be prepared to be the largest force contributor to such operations

[789] Our planning needs to acknowledge that we could be called upon to undertake several operations simultaneously, as we are at present in East Timor, Bougainville and the Solomon Islands

[790] Resisting Aggression In the highly unlikely event of unprovoked armed aggression against any of our immediate neighbours, Australia would want to be in a position, if asked and if we concluded that the scale of our interests and the seriousness of the situation warranted such action, to help our neighbours defend themselves

[791] The capability to provide such help would be drawn from the forces we have developed for the defence of Australia

[792] Fortunately the strategic geography of our neighbourhood makes this feasible

[793] All of our immediate neighbours are island and archipelagic states

[794] Their defences against external aggression would - like Australia’s - rely heavily on the ability to control their air and sea approaches and prevent hostile forces from approaching their shores

[795] The air and naval capabilities we develop for the defence of Australia would be able to make a valuable contribution to this task

[796] Land forces would also be important for securing bases, and providing specific contributions such as special forces

[797] We would provide such support only at the request of a neighbouring government, and would expect to be able to operate from bases in its territory

[798] We would also expect that other regional countries would provide support, most probably in the form of a coalition operating under a UN or international mandate

[799] Lower-level Operations 6.14 Lower-level operations, such as evacuations, disaster relief and peacekeeping operations of different sorts, are the most likely types of operation that we might need to undertake in our immediate neighbourhood

[800] The ADF needs to be prepared and equipped to undertake such operations should it be required to do so

[801] In general, the capabilities we develop in the ADF for defending Australia provide forces appropriate for these tasks

[802] However, the experiences of East Timor, Bougainville, Cambodia and elsewhere have taught us important lessons about the use of forces in lower level contingencies

[803] Such operations have specific characteristics that place strong demands on some elements of the ADF especially our land forces, logistics capacity and deployment capabilities

[804] Key lessons and their implications for our forces include the following. Training

[805] Operations like INTERFET and the Peace Monitoring Group on Bougainville place great demands on the training and personal quality of the men and women of the ADF

[806] In sometimes dangerous and ambiguous situations, they can be called upon to make snap judgments that can have life and death significance

[807] Often these decisions must be made on the spot by junior personnel who must have the training, preparation and personal qualities to handle such situations

[808] Readiness and Sustainment

[809] The need for operations such as evacuations or support for a legitimate government can arise quickly and with little warning, so forces need to be available at high levels of readiness

[810] Some kinds of operations, such as peacekeeping, can require a relatively large presence on the ground, so significant numbers of personnel might need to be deployed and supported

[811] And while some types of operations such as evacuations are over quickly, others, such as some types of peacekeeping, can last for months or even years

[812] So it is important that forces are sized and structured to allow sustainment and rotation

[813] Deployment and Support

[814] Forces may need to be inserted, and evacuees extracted, sometimes in dangerous circumstances, so substantial military air and sealift must be available

[815] Forces must be supported and provided with a wide range of services in difficult conditions

[816] There is often a need to provide relief services to local populations as well

[817] We therefore need adequate logistics and support capabilities, including deployable medical facilities, cargo-handling systems, water and fuel supply facilities, and engineering capabilities

[818] Firepower and mobility

[819] Success in pacifying an unstable situation often depends on a demonstrated ability and willingness to use preponderant force swiftly in response to any violence, so forces need to have ample firepower

[820] While such operations might typically involve lightly-armed adversaries, there can be potential for escalation by intervention of well-armed conventional forces

[821] We need to have capabilities available to deter or, if need be, respond to such escalation

[822] This would often involve not just land force capabilities but also air and naval forces to protect force elements as they deploy, maintain lines of supply and provide additional firepower

[823] Command and Communications

[824] The task of leading such operations places additional demands on the ADF for command, communications, intelligence and other facilities

[825] The Government intends that, within the capabilities we develop for the defence of Australia, we will make sure that we have sufficient forces to meet these demands, so that we are well prepared to respond to credible contingencies in our immediate neighbourhood

[826] At the same time, it is important that we recognise the limits to Australia’s ability to influence and help in major crises, even in our immediate neighbourhood

[827] Relatively small crisis situations can require very high levels of resources to manage and control

[828] For example, even if we had had much larger forces than we have today, Australia could not have undertaken to restore peace and security in East Timor under INTERFET except with the help of a large number of coalition partners and the cooperation of the Indonesian authorities

[829] Australia would contemplate contributing with armed forces to an international response in our immediate region only if it had the support of our neighbours, and of other countries from the region and beyond

[830] More broadly, our approach to lower level regional crises needs to recognise that the use of armed forces is not always a useful or practical response to a crisis

[831] The Government has developed a set of guidelines for commitment of the ADF to lower level operations

[832] These conditions include: the nature and extent of Australia’s interests, including strategic, political, humanitarian and alliance issues; whether the mission has a clear mandate, goals and end-point; whether the mission's goals are achievable in all the circumstances and with the resources available; the extent of international support for the mission; costs of Australian participation, including the effect on the ADF’s capacity to perform other tasks; training and other benefits to the ADF; risks to personnel involved; and consequences for Australia’s wider interests and international relationships

[833] The third priority for Australia’s forces is to be able to contribute effectively to international coalitions of forces to meet crises beyond our immediate neighbourhood where our interests are engaged

[834] Such coalitions might involve operations ranging from peacekeeping and disaster relief to relatively high-intensity conflict

[835] In general, the closer a crisis to Australia, the larger the contribution we would want to be able to provide

[836] We do not envisage that Australia would commit forces to operations beyond our immediate neighbourhood except as part of a multinational coalition

[837] The scale of our contribution would depend on a wide range of factors, but in general we would expect to make a greater contribution to coalition operations closer to home, where our interests and responsibilities are greater

[838] In Southeast Asia we would want to be able to make a substantial contribution to any regional coalition that we decided to support - especially if it involved our undertakings under the FPDA

[839] In the wider Asia Pacific region we would want to have the capacity to make a significant contribution to any coalition we thought it appropriate to join

[840] In most cases the United States would lead such a coalition, and we would expect our forces to operate closely with US forces

[841] Beyond the Asia Pacific region we would normally consider only a relatively modest contribution to any wider UN or US-led coalition, proportionate to our interests and the commitments of contributors from elsewhere in the world

[842] We would be most unlikely to contemplate the leadership of any coalition operations that were focused beyond Southeast Asia or the South Pacific

[843] We would apply similar criteria to those outlined above in paragraph 6.18 to deciding whether or not to commit forces in particular circumstances

[844] The Government would always place high priority on force protection arrangements for Australian contingents to ensure that no unnecessary risks were taken with Australian lives

[845] We would expect to be able to provide the forces needed to contribute to coalition operations from within the capabilities we develop for the defence of Australia and for operations within our immediate region

[846] The key requirements of such forces would be that they should be able to succeed with an acceptable level of risk in the operational environment expected, taking into account the levels of adversary forces and capabilities that they might encounter

[847] They should also be capable of operating adequately with the other coalition members

[848] In broad terms, these conditions suggest that a major Australian contribution to a coalition for higher intensity operations would more likely involve air or naval forces than land forces

[849] The air and naval forces we develop for the defence of Australia will provide the Government with a range of options to contribute to coalitions in higher intensity operations against well-armed adversaries

[850] Our land forces would be ideally suited to provide contributions to lower intensity operations including peace-enforcement, peacekeeping and many types of humanitarian operations

[851] Such operations are much more likely than high intensity operations and would emphasise mobility and the levels of protection and firepower appropriate for our own environment, rather than the kinds of heavy armoured capabilities needed for high intensity continental warfare

[861] Capability Priorities for the ADF 6.29 The Government has determined that in order to achieve these strategic tasks, the ADF will maintain and further develop an integrated and balanced joint force that can provide two key sets of capabilities

[862] First, Australia will maintain maritime capabilities - mostly air and naval forces - that can defend Australia by denying our air and sea approaches to any credible hostile forces

[865] These forces will also have the capability to contribute substantially to supporting the security of our immediate neighbourhood, and to contribute to coalition operations further afield, in lower intensity operations

[866] Capability Development Principles

[867] The priorities set out in the preceding paragraphs are the basis on which the Government has taken decisions about the development of the ADF’s capabilities

[868] Those decisions have been guided by the following principles

[869] Nothing can remove the element of the unexpected from our military affairs

[870] Over the next decade, we can be reasonably sure that governments will consider using the ADF in circumstances that we have not envisaged

[871] So our defence planning should not leave us with a set of capabilities that is too narrowly focused on specific scenarios

[872] Our aim is to provide Australia with a set of capabilities that will be flexible enough to provide governments with a range of military options across a spectrum of credible situations within the priorities set out in this Chapter

[873] Capability is much more than just a piece of equipment

[874] It includes everything that contributes to the ADF’s ability to achieve a particular result at a particular time

[875] That means it encompasses personnel and their training, support and maintenance, logistics, intelligence, doctrine, and many other contributing elements

[876] It draws on the wider national infrastructure, including the key role of industry

[877] It also means that different types of capability themselves need to work together to achieve larger results

[878] The ways in which different elements of capability, and different capabilities themselves, work together are critical to the effective conduct of operations

[879] We have therefore sought to consider how the elements of the ADF can best work together to provide an integrated set of capabilities

[880] Interoperability The same factors place a premium on interoperability between our forces and those of our allies and other countries with whom we might want to operate in coalition

[881] The development of interoperability - within limits of cost and other factors - is an important issue in capability development

[882] We need to be realistic about the amount of warning we might receive of the need to use our armed forces

[883] Crises do not usually occur without some indication, so we do not need to keep our forces constantly at the highest pitch of readiness for operations

[884] That would be very expensive, and it would erode our capabilities because forces held at very high readiness cannot undertake the full range of training and exercising

[885] But - except in the case of a major attack on Australia - we cannot assume that we would receive enough warning of a crisis to allow us to build major new capabilities, or significantly expand or enhance existing ones

[886] It would take several years at least to replace or upgrade our key platforms and systems, and to train the people to operate and support them

[887] The Government therefore believes that Australia should maintain in a fully developed form the capabilities that would be necessary to achieve key tasks

[888] Our aim is to establish a sustained long-term program to develop and upgrade our forces progressively so they retain the required level of capability without the need for crash programs

[889] Capability Edge The Government believes that Australia’s defence planning should aim to provide our forces with a clear margin of superiority against any credible adversary

[890] We will plan to give our forces a high chance of success and survival

[891] To achieve that we need to ensure that, working together, our forces have an overall capability edge in key roles

[892] We have taken account of the expected trend of capability developments in the region over the next 10 years and beyond, and have planned for sustained improvements in our capabilities to meet those expected trends

[893] We have not based our planning on the forces or capabilities of any particular country or set of countries, but have looked at capability trends throughout our wider region

[894] Maintaining a capability edge will not be easy

[895] In future we will no longer be able to rely, as we have in the past, on an assumption that either our technology or our trained people will be decisively better than those of other regional forces

[896] Our focus, even more than at present, will be on the advantages we can achieve by combining well-trained people with the effective use of technology - what we have called the ‘knowledge edge’

[897] Our capability edge will also come from the innovative ways in which we develop our doctrine, organisation and logistics

[898] Problems often come together, so it is important that our Defence Force should be able to do more than one thing at a time

[899] But Australia cannot maintain forces that would allow us to do all the things we might want to be able to do concurrently in different locations

[900] We therefore do not plan on the ability to undertake major operations simultaneously in more than one theatre of operations

[901] However, within a theatre of operation, especially in the defence of Australia, we would need forces large enough to undertake some types of operation simultaneously in widely separated locations

[902] And in our immediate region, we may need to be able to sustain one major deployment and undertake a lesser deployment at the same time

[903] We must be able to sustain our forces once deployed

[904] A sustainable force calls for different things: the ability to replace combat units in sufficient time and the capacity to supply and support forces operating at high tempos

[905] It therefore needs an effective long-term approach to recruiting and retaining our people, and it needs a capable industry base to draw on for support

[906] The Defence Capability Plan places a high priority on maximising the opportunities offered by the information technology revolution to make our forces more effective

[907] Information capabilities have been highlighted in a separate capability grouping to ensure that they receive proper attention and prominence, but in reality information systems will be profoundly important in the development of all our capabilities

[908] For Australia, effective exploitation of information capabilities will be critical to maintaining our edge

[909] More broadly, we have given close attention to the potential for revolutionary changes in the nature of warfare and the composition of armed forces flowing from current and prospective technological innovations

[910] We have balanced two imperatives - on the one hand, to keep open opportunities to take advantage of major redirections in technology as they emerge and, on the other hand, to sustain the pace of development of our forces to take advantage of technologies already available

[911] Our guiding principle has been to plan to exploit technologies that are now under active development, but not to defer investment where no new alternative is now in prospect

[912] Where possible, we have sought to allow time for new technologies to evolve before committing ourselves

[913] More discussion of our approach to the future of technology in our capabilities is provided in Chapter Ten

[914] The Government has also taken account of the lessons of the Collins submarine project for the approach we should take, in conjunction with Australian industry, to the development of high-technology capabilities

[915] The difficulties with the Collins project should not deter us from developing high-technology capabilities, because they are essential to our ability to defend Australia

[916] But they should caution us against taking unnecessary technical risks in developing new systems where existing ones may be adequate, and to ensure that risks are better understood and managed in future projects

[917] These lessons are being applied in the reform of our acquisition processes that is now under way

[918] This reform program and our approach to effective partnering with industry are described in Chapter Nine

[919] The Government has sought at all times to provide the maximum capability at the lowest possible cost by seeking the most cost-effective solutions available

[920] For example, we are looking for ways to minimise the number of different types of platforms and systems that the ADF operates, in order to save training, operating and support costs

[921] This has had an important impact on our approach to the planning of some types of capability

[1104] The Defence Capability Plan A New Approach To Capability Planning

[1106] In doing this we have developed a new approach to defence capability planning

[1107] Our aim is to provide the Defence Force with clear, long-term goals for the development of our armed forces, and the funding needed to achieve those goals

[1108] We have called this the Defence Capability Plan

[1109] The Defence Capability Plan will not remain immutable over the next decade

[1110] It will be reviewed annually to take account of changing strategic circumstances, new technologies and changed priorities

[1111] Individual projects will need to be considered and approved by government before proceeding

[1112] Nevertheless, the Capability Plan will provide a clear basis for decisions now and into the future

[1554] Human Dimensions
