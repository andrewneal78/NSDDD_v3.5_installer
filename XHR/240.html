[71] CHAPTER I THE STATE OF NICARAGUA A. PROCESS OF FORMATION OF THE NATION-STATE Before 1523, the Pacific and central regions of what is now Nicaragua were occupied by different indigenous groups, coming from the north, organized into small political units, none of which can be considered a State

[72] The Spanish conquest and colonization led to the formation of the territory of the Province of Nicaragua, populated by indigenous people, Spaniards, Black people, and people of mixed blood, under the control of the Spanish monarchy

[73] For almost three hundred years, the Province, with its capital in León, was part of that State in which all powers: Executive, Legislative and Judicial, were concentrated in the person of the King

[74] At the end of the 18th century and beginning of the 19th century, a desire for separatism emerged in some sectors of the colonial aristocracy, which was favored by European events and the independence movements in other regions of America

[75] The transition from the colonial regime to the nation-state was a long process of six decades (1798-1858) that began at the end of the 18th century, when the economic decline of the Spanish Empire motivated landowners and merchants in the province of Nicaragua to become aware that the colonial system represented a brake on their strategy development

[76] Independence allowed for the free export of agricultural products, thus breaking the Spanish monopoly

[77] By the end of the 18th century and the beginning of the 19th century, a certain historical consciousness already existed among the local Creole elite, arising from the marginalized situation in which they lived within the colonial system, as well as from their own development prospects

[78] In 1821, Nicaragua separated from Spain

[79] Thus the monarchical State disappeared in the Province, the problem was how to replace it

[80] The urgency of creating a new legitimacy and establishing organs of control and coercion arose in the midst of a society that for centuries had ignored any autonomy and had remained united around the legitimacy of royal majesty

[81] The political rupture brought about a social rupture and the overflow of forces that had until then been kept under pressure

[82] After independence, Nicaragua was immersed in a constant state of violence due to struggles between the dominant families vying for power

[83] In particular, the elites of León and Granada clashed in a struggle for hegemony, which was one of the main causes of the delay in the formation of a national state

[430] The political, economic, social, technological, and ideological changes that have occurred in the international system since 1990 have necessitated a redefinition of security and defense concepts and frameworks around the world, different from those that existed during the Cold War

[431] The intensification of the globalization process and its effects have contributed to the proliferation and spread of threats worldwide in relatively short periods of time, largely facilitated by the extraordinary development of communications and easy access to information

[432] Added to this are the changes that the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, in the United States of America brought about in the configuration of the multilateral security agenda, as well as in the nature of the threat to international stability and how it is perceived by States

[433] Peace and conflict situations have been significantly affected by various factors, including: The end of bipolarity (expressed in the East-West confrontation on the ideological, political, military and economic levels

[434] I The efforts to build a new military order

[435] I The limited progress in disarmament and non-proliferation regimes for weapons of mass destruction, particularly nuclear weapons

[436] The consolidation of the globalization process through the formation of large economic blocs in America, Europe and Asia

[437] I The action of new forces and actors with a multiplicity of motivations (political, ideological, ethnic, religious, economic, social, criminal, technological and cultural) at the national, regional and international levels

[438] I The transformation and classification of low-profile transnational illicit acts in the international Cold War scenario, which are now considered non-traditional threats and priorities for international and state security

[439] In this context, the State remains the form of political and legal organization par excellence, and is the central actor in international relations

[440] In some parts of the world, traditional conflict scenarios resulting from interstate disputes of various kinds and conflicting national interests still exist. This will continue to be a reality and a key reference for the development of security and defense policies in all countries

[441] At the beginning of the present century, global security problems have been organized into three broad categories: Those linked to interstate relations and which are called problems of the traditional or conventional security agenda (territorial disputes, interference in the internal affairs of other States, border disputes, among others

[442] Intra-state conflicts, fundamentally associated with strong ethnic-religious antagonisms and almost always fostered by separatist pretensions and fundamentalist positions

[443] II The so-called emerging security problems, related to the phenomenon of globalization and whose origins, actors and forms of solution are not exclusively a state matter, but rather require the inclusion and participation of various actors in the international system (International Organizations, Non-Governmental Organizations, Pressure Groups, among others), which demand higher levels of cooperation

[444] This is what is commonly called the new security agenda

[445] Today, the permeability of state borders—a product of extraordinary technological and communications advancements, measures aimed at deregulating and liberalizing international trade, and the implementation of provisions limiting state controls—has given rise to what are identified as non-traditional security threats or transnational threats

[446] From this perspective, the security-threatening problems associated with this new vision are numerous: the proliferation of weapons of mass destruction, transnational organized crime, drug trafficking, illicit trafficking in arms and people, international terrorism, and money laundering, among the most notable

[447] All of these threats are interrelated and mutually reinforcing, requiring international intergovernmental cooperation

[448] These threats, regardless of their place of origin or geographic location of maximum concentration, produce effects that cannot be contained within national borders, as they easily transcend the boundaries of a single state and have consequences within others. They affect social order and governance, impact economic plans and other public policies, and therefore require comprehensive responses

[449] Governments are attempting to provide solutions through the adoption of policies and determined international cooperation, which takes various forms: technical, scientific, and financial assistance. In cases requiring an operational response, they are coordinating collective efforts to exercise new security and defense functions, through which they resort to forms and models of international coordination in these areas

[450] At the same time, a progressive period of international law is developing, within the United Nations and within the framework of various regional organizations

[451] This process is evident, among other things, in the considerable production of international legislation aimed at defining actions and instruments to operationally counter non-traditional threats and minimize the potential for traditional conflicts

[452] In the military sphere, progress is being made in the formation of strategic coalitions as a mechanism for globally addressing the demands of collective security

[453] Likewise, many states have reformulated their national, regional, and hemispheric security and defense doctrines to address global demands in these areas in a timely manner

[454] The concept of security as we know it today has focused attention on its multidimensional nature

[455] In this regard, at least six basic dimensions of security are identified: military, political, economic, social, legal, and environmental

[456] Consequently, the concept of security has shifted its focus from a narrow and specific concept, associated with the security of the State and therefore the preservation of its sovereignty, political independence, and territorial integrity, to a broader and more comprehensive definition

[457] Thus, in the 1980s and particularly the 1990s, the debate on international security expanded to include categories such as global security, democratic security, and human security

[458] At the beginning of this century, the terrorist attacks on the World Trade Center and the Pentagon on September 11, 2001, marked a turning point in the international system and resulted in a restructuring of global alliances, where the global fight against terrorism has become the primary objective of a specific group of countries, even above the priorities and strategic realities of other states and the particularities of each region

[459] In this sense, the effectiveness of multilateral organizations and joint efforts as a means of responding to the countless challenges posed by the terrorist threat has been limited by the predominance of marked political and military unilateralism in addressing this issue

[460] The recent wars in Afghanistan and the Persian Gulf demonstrated that military conflicts will be present in the future as they have been in the past, that situations of antagonism and friction that lead to conflict are still present, and that defense cannot be forgotten as an indispensable constituent element of security to confront even the so-called new threats

[897] PEACEKEEPING OPERATIONS They are an instrument that the UN has implemented in response to conflict situations that threaten international peace and security, to provide lasting solutions and/or alleviate their consequences

[898] They can be defined as multinational, legitimate operations carried out under the auspices of the UN, involving military forces, diplomatic and humanitarian agencies, and whose objective is to eliminate threats to international peace and security by employing peaceful means or limiting the use of force

[899] Although the concept and classification of peace operations are not clearly defined in the United Nations Charter, experience allows us to differentiate two important categories: Peacekeeping, regulated in Chapter VI of the UN Charter and whose fundamental characteristic lies in the non-use of coercive force (supported only in cases of self-defense); and Peace Enforcement, governed by the provisions of Chapter VII of the Charter and contemplating the use of coercive measures and actions

[900] In accordance with the fundamental principles of the Political Constitution, which state that "international relations are based on friendship and solidarity among peoples and reciprocity among States; as well as the peaceful settlement of international disputes by the means provided by International Law," the Nicaraguan State conceives Peacekeeping Operations as a multinational effort to achieve peace and international stability, through the deployment of civilian and military resources under the command and control of the United Nations and with the consent of the parties to the conflict, to carry out actions primarily related to humanitarian aid, such as: medical care for the affected population, humanitarian demining actions, certification and destruction of weapons and unexploded ordnance, as well as security and supply actions

[901] Consequently, the State of Nicaragua defines its participation in peacekeeping operations solely as those carried out in accordance with Chapter VI of the United Nations Charter, and primarily those of a strictly humanitarian nature, in accordance with the procedures established in this Charter

[1006] HUMANITARIAN DEMINING AS STATE POLICY

[1007] The State of Nicaragua embraces the principle of International Humanitarian Law, which stipulates that the right of parties to an armed conflict to choose the methods or means of combat is not unlimited

[1008] It also recognizes the principles prohibiting the use, in armed conflict, of weapons, projectiles, materials, and methods of combat that are likely to cause superfluous injury or unnecessary suffering, as well as the need to distinguish between civilians and combatants

[1009] With these principles and commitments to hemispheric and international peace as a reference, Nicaragua signed the Convention on the Prohibition of the Use, Stockpiling, Production and Transfer of Anti-Personnel Mines and on Their Destruction (Ottawa Convention) on December 5, 1997, and ratified it on October 2, 1998, entering into force on October 9 of that same year

[1010] Demining in Nicaragua is a fundamentally humanitarian task, recognizing the State's unwavering commitment to providing medical care, social reintegration, and productive reintegration for surviving victims of mine accidents, and to developing the necessary efforts to prevent future accidents alongside the mine clearance and destruction work carried out by the Nicaraguan Army's sappers

[1011] From this perspective, national plans and projects related to demining are based on the Policy on Mine Clearance and Destruction and the Policy for Comprehensive Care for Victims and Survivors of Mines or War Devices, formulated by the National Demining Commission (CND) and contained in the National Demining Program

[1012] This policy has important components that contribute to the development of rural areas affected by the armed conflict, the consolidation of democracy, and the promotion of mutual trust

[1013] At the international level, Nicaragua actively advocates in multilateral forums for the universalization of the Ottawa Convention and the total eradication of the use, manufacture, and transfer of mines

[1014] Consequently, the successful implementation of this State Policy requires the concerted effort of state institutions, civil society, and the international community, which is carried out under the coordination of the CND

[1015] MINE CLEARANCE AND DESTRUCTION POLICIES Nicaragua adopts and incorporates into its national legislation, as a matter of State Policy, the commitment to ensure the destruction of mines installed in the national territory, and the obligation to never, under any circumstances: use antipersonnel mines; develop, produce, acquire in any way or other way, stockpile, conserve, or transfer to anyone, directly or indirectly, antipersonnel mines; nor assist, encourage, or induce in any way or another a State Party to the Ottawa Convention to participate in any activity prohibited

[1016] The State of Nicaragua recognizes that its primary responsibility is to ensure the safety and physical integrity of its citizens, and also considers it a duty, if it were able to do so, to provide assistance for mine clearance and related activities to any country, organization, or global or regional institution that requests it, through official bodies

[1301] Foundation of the Sandinista Popular Army (EPS) and first steps in its institutionalization (1979-1984) On July 19, 1979, the government of Anastasio Somoza Debayle was overthrown by armed insurrection and the Government Junta of National Reconstruction (JGRN) was installed, which proceeded to sanction the Fundamental Statute of the Republic of Nicaragua, as a provisional supreme norm, repealed the old formal structures of power and laid the foundations for the new institutional order

[1302] The executive, legislative, and judicial powers were transferred to the JGRN, the Council of State, and the Courts of Justice, respectively

[1303] The Statute dissolved the National Guard (GN), the National Security Office, and the Military Intelligence Service (SIM), repealed the entire legal system that governed them, and provided for the replacement of the GN, according to Article 24, "by a new National Army of a patriotic character, dedicated to the defense of the democratic process and the sovereignty and independence of the Nation, as well as the integrity of its territory

[1304] By Decree No. 53 of August 22, 1979, the JGRN decreed the creation of the Sandinista Popular Army (EPS) as the sole armed force of the Republic

[1305] Through Decree No. 54 of September 18, the main commanders of the EPS were appointed and a process of forming military structures throughout the country began. The Command and General Staff, the Sandinista Air Force, and territorial units were organized, integrated based on the guerrilla forces

[1306] The Sandinista Popular Militias were created on February 21, 1980, with the goal of institutionalizing citizen participation in defense and serving as a complementary force to the permanent units. Likewise, on August 13 of the same year, the Sandinista Navy was formed to exercise sovereignty on maritime borders

[1307] Between 1980 and 1982, several institutional steps took place that completed the legal framework within which the new armed forces would develop: the Law on the Organization of Military Audit and Provisional Military Criminal Procedure, the Provisional Law on Military Crimes, Disciplinary Regulations, combat regulations, and other provisions governing military life

[1308] The initial territorial organic structure adopted by the EPS from 1979 to 1981 consisted of seven military regions comprising the 16 departments according to the political and administrative division established by the Government

[1309] During its initial stage (1979-1983), the number of EPS troops grew as the level of organization of the irregular forces opposed to the revolutionary process and the danger of their actions increased, which went from border attacks to larger-scale operations

[1310] On September 13, 1983, the JGRN passed the Patriotic Military Service Law to address the armed conflict

[1311] b. The EPS, the military doctrine of the Revolution and the beginning of the peace process (1985-1988) In 1985, the military doctrinal basis of the revolutionary State was concluded, which had as its fundamental axis the defense of the Revolution against the actions of irregular forces

[1312] The objective was to lead them to a situation of strategic defeat through the conception of irregular warfare and the disposition of the State's capabilities against a probable direct foreign aggression, constituting the EPS as the nucleus around which the different sectors of the population would gather to combat, resist, bog down, wear down, and expel the opposing forces from the national territory, through the strategic conception of the "war of the entire people

[1313] In the process of institutionalizing the EPS, the Law Creation the Grades of Honor, Positions and Military Ranks was reformed in 1986, increasing the ranks from seven military ranks to eleven conventional ranks, from second lieutenant to general of the army

MILITARY STRATEGY The National Strategy of Nicaragua is expressed in two levels: Political Strategic and Military Strategy, both conceived as part of the National Security Strategy, which can be defined as the art of directing, guiding and coordinating all the energies and forces of the nation towards the achievement of national objectives

[1508] The National Security Strategy is directed by the highest authorities of the Nicaraguan State and involves all of the nation's forces

[1509] The Military Strategy is, in turn, an integral and essential part of the National Security Strategy and is conceived as the art and science of employing the nation's armed forces to ensure the preservation of the National Defense objectives contained in State policies

[1510] This strategy is led by the President of the Republic in his capacity as Supreme Commander of the Nicaraguan Army, with political advice from the Minister of Defense and directed by the Commander-in-Chief of the Army

[1511] The strategic concept constitutes the expression of the decision adopted to ensure the achievement of military objectives

[1512] It includes the analysis of the strategic situation, the evaluation of one's own capabilities and threats and the definition of the principles and general lines that develop them, among the most important are the following: DETERRENCE It is a political-strategic modality of employment of the means of Defense in order to generate a discouraging effect on the hostile intentions of real or potential adversaries against national interests and objectives

[1513] Its effectiveness requires the indispensable participation of the Army, political consensus, and social support that unites the different components of National Power

[1514] The Nicaraguan Army plays a leading role in the organization and preparation of the National Defense system

[1515] In this sense, their participation is vital to generating the deterrent effect derived from the National Security Strategy

[1516] To achieve this, it is essential to maintain adequate military force to discourage the manifestation of hostile forces against the national territory

[1517] The State's deterrent power rests primarily on the firm and determined will of the population and its representatives within the political leadership bodies to defend national interests and objectives, using, in the first instance, the resources offered by international law and relying primarily on the instruments of our foreign and cooperation policy

[1518] This must be accompanied by the availability of human and material resources appropriate to the needs of the Defense, without such availability, under any circumstances, compromising the economic and social development of the nation

[1519] Collective and Solidarity Defense. It is manifested through compliance with agreements between neighboring countries that allow political and military advantages between the allies without undermining their sovereignty

[1520] The principles of Collective and Solidarity Defense are in correspondence with those of the Central American Integration System (SICA): a

[1521] Non-aggression against other states; common security (the security of one state is the security of all); collective and united defense in the event of aggression; strengthening the integration and unity of states; and respect for the purposes and principles of the charters of the UN, the OAS, and SICA

[1522] b. Sovereign equality among states and legal security in their relations

[1523] c. Peaceful settlement of disputes, respect for the sovereignty, independence and territorial integrity of member countries, self-determination of states

[1524] d. Solidarity and security with the peoples and governments of the area

[1525] Preventive activities of an organizational nature and mobilization capacity of forces, resources and assets of the national economy in proportion to the real or potential threats to National Security and the real possibilities of maintaining compliance with the provisions of the Law

[1526] It is characterized by the level and degree of training of the forces to be used quickly and opportunely according to the assigned missions

[1527] MILITARY PRESENCE Maintain a permanent deployment proportional to economic possibilities but capable of opposing the initial phases of a conflict as combat security, avoiding the loss of territory and allowing the operational deployment of the main forces for the defense of sovereignty, independence and territorial integrity

[1528] This presence is directly proportional in each location to the real possibility of a conflict developing or the risk of aggression, and is as limited as possible in those places where the risk is less likely

[1529] MILITARY GENERALITIES Military capabilities are determined by technical, material, budgetary and human possibilities, and are manifested in the type of military operations by the parameters or indices of maneuver, transportation, multilateral assurance, and support of the forces deployed in the territory for the time necessary in fulfillment of missions that correspond to them by their nature

[1530] The Nicaraguan Army has adequate military capabilities that allow it to successfully fulfill its missions, primarily the defense of sovereignty, independence, and territorial integrity as its fundamental mission, and incorporating nontraditional missions, determined by new threats to national interests and the protection of the population and its resources from the effects of natural or man-made phenomena

[1531] Rigid systems of action in the face of a defined threat are no longer valid; a flexible structure and procedures are needed to respond appropriately to any risk, threat, or attack of any kind

[1532] For this reason, the Nicaraguan Army is structured around an adequate organization of command and forces, allowing it to realize its potential in times of peace and war, based on its men and women, whose training and professionalism make them the mainstay of its institutional capabilities

[1533] Given the limitations of technical and material resources to meet the multiple needs of defense and the demands of the population, a permanent and effective use of human and material resources is necessary

[1535] I With a high level of training

[1536] With an adequate provision of the necessary means to successfully face the traditional and non-traditional missions required by National Security and Defense

[1537] I With the possibility of timely increasing its capabilities with resources from national mobilization

[1538] I Capable of contributing to the deterrence of potential adversaries or to victory in the event that war is inevitable

[1726] DEFENSE AS A PUBLIC GOOD One of the main complexities in planning National Security is providing or producing Defense in an effective and efficient manner

[1729] DEFENSE AS AN INVESTMENT Defense must be viewed by all sectors of society through the prism of the nation, focusing on protecting the resources allocated to Defense and enabling the development of a military strategy with the economic effort that this entails

[1730] This goes beyond purely economic considerations, as it is fundamentally a political decision linked to the existence, stability, and development of the nation

[1731] In Nicaragua, investment in defense is in line with a State Policy that defines national objectives and interests, including the need to guarantee national security and, consequently, a sustainable, firm, and lasting peace

[1732] Thus, the benefit of budget allocations to Defense is intended to promote the country's governability and development

[1733] We Nicaraguans view defense as an investment, as it constitutes a security asset that safeguards the nation's sovereignty, independence, and territorial integrity against traditional and non-traditional threats
