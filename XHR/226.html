[218] By doing so, the actor (for example, the Kingdom of the Netherlands, or an international organisation such as the UN, NATO or the EU) can put its security policy into practice

[219] By employing political, diplomatic, economic, socio-cultural, humanitarian and military activities, influence will be exerted or power exercised for the purpose of realising these objectives

[220] A state’s means of power are also referred to as instruments of national power

[221] These can be divided into the following categories (DIME): - the diplomatic instrument, - information as an instrument, - the military instrument, - the economic instrument

[222] The diplomatic instrument is the means used by a state or an international organisation to establish and maintain relations with foreign powers and other actors on the international stage and to protect its interests and realise its objectives

[223] Diplomacy is used to try to influence other actors

[224] Diplomatic pressure can also be applied, with or without the overt threat of the use of other means of power

[225] International fora are an important stage upon which actors use their diplomatic might

[226] A state or international actor wields its diplomatic power through political leaders and ambassadors

[227] Other means of diplomatic power include the use of special envoys, negotiations, participation in or boycot of alliances or coalitions or the signing of treaties

[236] The military instrument can be used by a government or a coalition of governments as a means to persuade other actors to take a particular course of action or to refrain from one

[241] The use of the military instrument alone cannot bring about a lasting solution to a conflict

[243] It is important, therefore, that military planners and commanders consider a security issue in its entirety; the effect of any integrated use of the military instrument can then be estimated

[245] By contributing to the successful use of other instruments of power, the military instrument plays a supporting and enabling role

[247] Viewed from an international perspective, the monopoly of the use of force does not necessarily lie with a military or police force

[281] Conflicts of interest, confrontations, crises and conflicts

[282] In the (inter)national arena, all actors always have their own interests and objectives

[283] A conflict of interests arises if a party’s interests and objectives are at odds with those of other actors

[284] If that is the case, and actors persist in these differences, the situation becomes one of confrontation

[285] Confrontations can harden and result in a crisis or a conflict

[286] To ensure an accurate definition, these terms are explained below

[287] In a conflict of interest or confrontation, there is an escalating difference of opinion about objectives that is so crucial that neither party will concede

[288] The root of the difference of opinion normally lies in conflicting economic or political interests, and it is in that sphere that the solution must initially be sought

[289] Nevertheless, the threat or actual use of fighting power is sometimes made, and this would normally serve to protect, prevent or deter. - Protection of national interests

[290] This includes, for example, airspace and border security, the military contribution to the coastguard and law enforcement on the high seas, as well as operations to counter smuggling and piracy

[292] In this case, military deployment supports the use of other instruments

[293] Examples are military diplomacy, such as showing the flag and military visits, and the provision of military advice, assistance, training to friendly nations and any necessary deterrence. - Deterrence

[294] Here, military deployment is intended to convince other actors of the willingness to use the military instrument and to apply coercion

[295] Examples are the forward deployment of military capabilities and the execution of joint exercises in or near the area in question

[296] The emphasis lies on the presence of military power; the actual use of force is not usually relevant here

[297] Confrontations may last a very long time, such as the Cold War, for example

[298] In conflicts of interest and confrontations, it is crucial that the situation does not escalate and develop into a crisis or conflict

[299] This applies particularly in the case of vital interests, where the level of damage caused by an escalation may be disproportionately high or even irreversible

[300] In the case of vital interests, therefore, much emphasis will be placed on prevention and protection

[301] By taking preventive action, in which case the use of force or intervention in an impending conflict is not yet an issue, a state’s political leaders preserve the greatest freedom of choice for the application of its instruments of power

[302] A crisis is deemed to have arisen when a state or population group loses control of the situation because of a conflict of interests, a confrontation or an impending or worsening disaster

[303] Crises have no set pattern and each has its own dynamic and characteristics

[304] Generally speaking, a crisis does not arise because of a single specific event, but because of a series of events spread over time

[305] Multiple conflicts of interest are usually to blame for a crisis

[306] A disaster often leads to a crisis and can also be classed under the phenomenon of a crisis, as the characteristics of each bear close resemblance to each other

[307] It involves the severe disruption of public safety, usually caused by a single, brief and catastrophic event at a single location

[308] When a disaster occurs, a great number of people are put in grave danger, and there is extensive material damage or significant damage to the environment, all within a short space of time

[309] Disasters can be caused by human actions (major accidents) or by natural events (earthquakes, hurricanes, flooding, pandemics

[310] The socio-economic consequences of a disaster are often more catastrophic than the physical disaster itself

[311] In an armed conflict, the conflict of interests has escalated to such an extent that parties resort to the use of weapons to secure their interests

[312] A conflict may be small scale and local, but it can also lead to large-scale violence

[388] Dutch security policy in a historical context From the time the Kingdom of the Netherlands was established in 1815, the armed forces had two tasks: 1) defending the Kingdom in Europe and 2) upholding law and order in the colonies

[389] The first task was the responsibility of the Royal Netherlands Army and the Royal Netherlands Navy; the second was performed in the Dutch East Indies by the KNIL, the Royal Netherlands East Indies Army and the Colonial Navy (later named East Indies Military Navy

[390] In the Caribbean, the Netherlands Forces in Curaçao and the Netherlands Forces in Surinam were tasked with the defence of the colonies, and a maritime presence was ensured by the Royal Netherlands Navy

[391] The Dutch armies concentrated on territorial defence, on assisting with domestic law enforcement and, in the colonies, on pacification

[392] Expeditionary actions were mainly the responsibility of the navy and the marine corps, who ever since the years of the Dutch war of independence (1568-1648) had been engaged all over the world in the military protection of trade, gunboat diplomacy and showing the flag

[393] In strategic terms, the Netherlands maintained a policy of armed neutrality until the Second World War and its armed forces in Europe concentrated mainly on the ability to engage in a major conflict in the event of any violation of that neutrality

[394] The assumption was that the potential opponent, the armed forces of another European power, were likely to conduct ‘regular’ operations

[395] After the conquests of the 19th century, the colonial troops focused mainly on maintaining internal law and order

[396] Activities of the KNIL were focused primarily on conducting offensive operations in rebellious regions, combating indigenous guerrilla fighters and piracy

[397] In 1913-1914, the very first deployment of Dutch military personnel with a peace-building task took place as part of a multinational operation in Albania

[552] Netherlands Defence Doctrine 3.2 The international legal framework International law consists of writen and unwriten rules that govern relations between states and international organisations

[553] The deployment of the military instrument outside national borders must have a legal basis in international law

[554] The rule of thumb is that the Netherlands may not operate in another country without that country’s consent

[555] There are essentially two universal rules for this: the principle of sovereignty and the prohibition of force, as expressed in the UN Charter

[556] After looking at these two rules, this chapter will discuss several other legal principles as well as the laws and jurisdiction that apply during military operations

[557] The sovereignty principle

[558] According to this principle, the authority of a state does not depend on another, higher authority for actions within its own country

[559] Under the non-intervention principle, as an extension of the sovereignty principle, no state may intervene in the internal or external affairs of another

[560] Prohibition of the use of force Article 2, paragraph 4 of the UN Charter prohibits the use or the threat of military force in international relations

[561] This stipulation is intended to prevent states from taking military action in the territory of or against another state, either on their own initiative or on the basis of national legislation

[562] The use of force and the right to self-defence There are three generally accepted exceptions to the prohibition of the use of force

[563] The first legal basis is the right of individual or collective self-defence, recognised in Article 51 of the UN Charter

[564] The right to self-defence applies in the event of an (imminent) armed attack on a country by another state or by an organised armed group

[565] Leading on from that, it is also the case that states may, under strict conditions, protect their citizens and, if necessary, evacuate them from another country with the help of military forces

[566] One of the conditions that apply here is that the host nation is no longer willing or able to offer those citizens the necessary protection

[567] The second legal basis is authorisation of the use of force by the UN Security Council on the basis of Chapter VII of the UN Charter

[568] The third legal basis is an invitation from the host nation

[569] This third legal basis focuses primarily – but not exclusively – on the stationing of troops and training, or preparing for operations as part of the collective self-defence of the country in question (for example, the enhanced Forward Presence in Lithuania

[570] There is also discussion about other exceptions to the prohibition of force, in particular about allowing humanitarian intervention

[571] The Netherlands’ position is that military intervention in a humanitarian emergency can be justified on moral and political grounds

[572] This means that humanitarian intervention is permissible as a last resort in exceptional cases and under strict conditions

[573] However, this is not to say that humanitarian intervention constitutes a legal basis

[574] The consequence of the different legal bases is that the responsibilities and powers of military personnel can differ widely in each operation

[575] Laws and jurisdiction The international law that governs whether a state may use force should be seen separately from the laws and jurisdiction that govern the actual use of force

[576] The legal framework that applies in the case of deployment outside the Netherlands is different for each operation, and in some cases even for different areas or phases of the same operation

[577] For example, counterdrug and counterpiracy operations (law enforcement) could thus also be subject to the Criminal Code and the Code of Criminal Procedure

[578] In all cases, the way in which the assigned tasks are performed and the authority exercised must be in accordance with the protective provisions of international humanitarian law

[579] International humanitarian law International humanitarian law (IHL), jus in bello, as defined in the Geneva Conventions with the associated supplementary protocols, provides the framework in military operations

[580] The purpose of IHL is to find a balance between military necessity (the reality of the use of force) and humanity (the prevention of unnecessary suffering

[581] IHL grants powers and imposes restrictions to this effect

[582] The powers consist mainly of the right of combatants to take part in hostilities, while the restrictions mainly encompass rules for the methods and means of warfare and rules which govern the protection of people and goods

[583] IHL only applies officially if there is a situation of ‘armed conflict’

[584] Whether the situation is indeed one of armed conflict depends on actual events and not on declarations or political views of warring parties

[585] Even if IHL does not apply formally, Dutch and NATO policy is to use IHL protective provisions as a safety margin for operations by the Dutch armed forces

[586] This prevents any confusion that might arise in respect of the powers of an intervention force as a result of its changing status in a conflict area

[587] This status can after all range from armed enforcement of peace or enforcement of the rule of law and stability to assistance in rebuilding and alleviation of human suffering

[588] There are two types of armed conflict, namely that between two or more states (international armed conflict), and that between one or more states and one or more organised armed groups, or between these groups themselves (non-international armed conflict

[751] Netherlands Defence Doctrine Fighting power 4.1 Introduction The overall capability supplied by the armed forces to perform the main tasks assigned to them is known as fighting power

[752] This fighting power comprises not only personnel and equipment, but also less visible factors such as willingness, cohesion, leadership, doctrine, power of renewal, and so on

[753] Being able to apply doctrine requires insight into all of these factors

[754] Fighting power is expressed in a model (see Figure 4.1) which consists of a mental, physical and conceptual component

[755] This chapter describes the model, discussing it in relation to the operating environment, as well as the development and application of fighting power

[756] Both the environment and the application of fighting power make demands on the effectiveness and efficiency of the fighting power developed

[757] This means that the fighting power must be brought up to a certain level before it can be applied

[758] Fighting power in a historical context The term ‘fighting power’, consisting of a conceptual, a physical and a mental component, was developed as a concept in doctrine in the 1990s

[759] These three aspects were in themselves not new to the exercise of military power, but their manifestations and correlations did change in the course of time

[760] From the middle of the 19th century, the development of weapons technology was constant

[761] With the advent of automatic weapons, rapid-fire artillery, smokeless ammunition, armoured (steam)ships, railways, aircraft and tanks or ‘battle wagons’, the effectiveness, mobility and striking power of the armed forces grew

[762] The growing control that governments had on society also meant that more and more people and equipment could be mobilised

[763] Roughly there were three phases: 1) increased firepower (19th century), 2) increased mobility (first half of the 20th century) and 3) increased command and control capabilities and computerisation (late 20th century

[764] The introduction of conscript armies brought with it a need to develop a centrally formulated operating method

[765] In the course of the 19th and 20th centuries, countries developed their own doctrine, based on their own wartime experience and also particularly on the opinions of society

[766] The philosophies of traditional military thinkers such as Jomini and Von Clausewitz (land operations), Mahan and Corbet (maritime operations) and Douhet and Mitchell (air operations) still influence the conceptual thinking of today’s armed forces

[767] Netherlands Defence Doctrine The mental component traditionally plays an important role in determining fighting power

[768] In the 19th century, the main emphasis was on physical and mental capabilities, on self-sacrifice and on the ability to withstand heavy losses without losing the will to fight (and to win

[769] Over the course of the 20th century, the emphasis shifted away from physical sacrifice and more attention was focused on matters such as motivation, team spirit, training, justification and social acceptance and support

[770] Until afer the First World War, military theorists regarded the mental component as the deciding factor

[771] The country with the greatest resilience would ultimately win the war

[772] Based on experiences from 1914-1918, the emphasis shifted to the conceptual and the physical element

[773] The Second World War showed that it was indeed the physical component that ultimately decided the outcome

[774] After the Cold War, emphasis once again shifted more towards the conceptual component

[775] In the “new world order” armed forces had to cope with a variety of threats and challenges, and had to perform a wide diversity of tasks, such as pacification and stabilisation, military rule, peacekeeping and counterinsurgency

[776] Doctrines and lessons learned that were once formulated for that purpose were once again being studied and practised

[777] Military operations traditionally distinguished between tactics and strategy

[778] Tactics encompassed the activities on the batlefield; strategy included all other maters, such as the way in which batles had to be prepared and set up (the campaign

[779] In the 19th century, modern warfare increased in scale and complexity as a result of technological developments and improved national organisation

[780] The strategic level was increasingly the point at which the measures and objectives in preparation for war took shape

[781] In this way, room was created for an “operational level” in military deployment

[782] After the First World War, the Russian and German armed forces were the first to give this level a place in their military activities and doctrine

[783] After the Second World War, the following five levels of warfare were eventually identified, defined and institutionalised: the political-strategic, the military-strategic, the operational, the tactical and the technical level

[784] Until the end of the Cold War, the overlap between the levels was limited, although there were differences per Service

[785] In the context of the NATO defence task, the levels within the land forces were largely linked to the size of units

[786] The military-strategic level was the domain of the theatre commander, known in today’s terminology as the commander of the combined joint task force

[787] Army groups and armies were active at operational level and army corps (including the Dutch) at tactical level

[788] For the naval and air forces, it was the tasking that determined the level of operating rather than the size of the units

[789] The Royal Netherlands Navy, for example, focused on anti-submarine warfare in the North Atlantic Ocean

[790] The purpose of this task was to protect the sea lines of communication (SLOCs) that were crucial to NATO in military-strategic terms

[791] Because of the advance technological level of their anti-submarine warfare capabilities, certain navy units were assigned the strategic task of tracking Soviet SSBNs (nuclear-powered submarines equipped with ballistic missiles) and destroying them in the event of war

[792] The Royal Netherlands Air Force, on the other hand, was mainly a tactical air force

[793] Its squadrons of air defence fighters aimed to achieve (local) air superiority by intercepting enemy aircraft

[794] Its squadrons of fighter-bombers were designed for (offensive) tasks such as the elimination of enemy airfields and radar stations, interdiction behind the front line, air support to land forces and tactical (photo) reconnaissance

[795] Both clusters operated with other NATO air forces in an integrated (combined) chain of command

[796] After the Cold War, with the modular participation in peace missions and international interventions, the ever-decreasing size of deployed units and the increased possibilities for command and control (a result of the increasing quality of sensors and faster communication as a result of digitisation) caused the real-time interaction and overlap between the levels of warfare to increase

[797] As a result, the traditional demarcation between the levels became less clear-cut, and ‘networked’ operating methods developed

[798] At the same time, the digitisation of society and the armed forces brought about a new operating domain: cyberspace

[799] This domain also featured task-specific levels of warfare, from tactical to strategic, and a high degree of overlapping

[800] The forms of “unlimited”, “whole of society” or “hybrid” warfare (mixtures of regular and irregular operations, both open and covert, also in the cyberspace domain, targeting all segments of an enemy society) that were developed by China and Russia in particular after the end of the Cold War were also characterised by a high degree of mixing between the operating levels

[801] With limited means, small numbers of personnel and at long range, this method of postmodern, multi-domain warfare made it possible to achieve major effects, without necessarily using kinetic weapon systems

[802] The overlapping of the levels of warfare thus increased even further

[803] Netherlands Defence Doctrine 4.2 The components of fighting power Fighting power is the ability to conduct military operations in an optimum cohesive totality of functionalities and components

[804] It is more than just the availability of means (capacities); there must also be the willingness and ability to deploy these means (capability

[805] If this is properly developed, it then becomes fighting power, and capacities are elevated to capabilities

[806] The components cannot be seen in isolation; they all affect one another

[807] Sophisticated equipment is thus useless without highly motivated and well-trained personnel and without a well-considered, doctrine-based deployment method

[808] Mental component Conceptual component Physical component Warfare is an activity conducted by people

[809] The willingness to engage in combat when necessary plays a key role in that activity

[810] The mental component of military operations is thus a combination of four interrelated factors: motivation, leadership, responsible organisation of the deployment of available personnel and materiel, and perception

[811] The mental component is extremely important, for the very reason that military personnel usually operate in conditions that not only call for great mental resilience, but which are also highly demanding in terms of conduct and attitude in a mission area

[812] Such requirements could include cultural awareness, empathy and an open attitude, as well as the need to demonstrate appropriate initiative, creativity, and the mental attitude to exercise restraint in the use of force

[813] Motivation is the result of training, confidence in the equipment, effective leadership and management, strong discipline, self-respect, mutual respect and a good understanding of what is going on

[814] In practice, motivation is closely linked to high morale

[815] Motivation can be influenced by external factors, such as public opinion

[816] An individual’s belief that there is public support for deployment in a conflict, the objective and execution of the mission are seen as legitimate and the necessary materiel is seen to be available to support the military activities will contribute substantially to his or her willingness to fight

[817] The media play a key role in this respect

[818] Leadership The commander is the embodiment of – effective – leadership

[819] That gives commanders at all levels an important role in the mental component

[820] He is also responsible for the training prior to the mission

[821] During the mission, it is the commander who, as leader, ensures the execution of tasks through action, motivation, inspiration and the power to instil in his personnel the will to “go for it”

[822] On deployment, the commander is the face of the unit

[823] His actions will shape the image held by all the other members of the unit

[824] He is responsible for the actions of all individual military personnel under his command

[825] Organising the deployment responsibly The way in which a commander organises the deployment of his assets will influence the mental component

[826] By doing this responsibly, he will ensure that his personnel trust in their own abilities and in the organisation, and he will create a support base for the operation

[827] Responsible organisation of deployment means, amongst other things, that sufficient assets (personnel, materiel and funds) are available for the successful execution of an operation

[828] A support base is also necessary, among military personnel as well as the general population

[829] Thorough preparation, also in education and training, and good planning are organisational preconditions for success

[830] One important means of responsible organisation is the use of risk management

[831] Commanders should ensure a sound balance between set objectives and the risks involved in activities necessary to achieve those objectives

[832] That means that risks need to be identified, assessed and dealt with

[833] This helps to ensure a safe working environment for man and machine and the responsible pursuit of the set objectives

[834] Account should also be taken of the effect that deployment in a mission area, constant exposure to danger, often in difficult conditions and far from home, will have on individual military personnel

[835] Perception Perception is the subjective observation of an objective reality

[836] Perception is shaped by the view of the world and mindset that determine the specific way an actor observes, interprets and anticipates reality

[837] Perception, as a core element of the cognitive dimension, is therefore a filter through which an actor sees the world

[838] This filter is based on cultural, societal and religious aspects

[839] Influencing an actor’s view of the world will change his perception so that he will interpret a threat, challenge, or even his own weakness, in a different way and will also prepare for it differently

[840] How an actor should be influenced depends on the goal to be achieved and on the actor, who could be a friend, an enemy or a neutral party

[841] An actor’s world view can be influenced indirectly using physical means, but also directly in the information environment, for example through misleading reports or fake news, possibly as part of a strategic communication campaign

[842] The objective of influencing the cognitive dimension is to bring about a change in an actor’s will and behaviour

[843] Military deployment can only be effective if personnel are sufficiently motivated, trained and educated

[844] Despite advances in unmanned technology and developments in robotics, the human factor remains key

[845] The physical presence of military personnel is still necessary, as this is the only way to establish contact with the local population

[846] That personal contact is essential in gaining a good understanding of needs, expectations, intentions and local conditions

[847] Support among the local population can only be established through a physical presence: military personnel are thus relevant for more than just fighting

[848] Furthermore, it is only people who can respond quickly to changing circumstances, read emotions and act in the spirit of the commander

[849] The physical component The people and means of an armed force form the physical component of fighting power; these are the personnel and materiel that are organised for deployment in an operation

[850] The terms ‘combat readiness’ and ‘mission readiness’ are used for the planning, preparation and deployment

[851] The way in which the armed forces are deployed determines the right composition, size and quality of the physical component

[852] Deployment options form the basis for requirements and instruction and training programmes

[853] With the materiel supplied, deployed personnel must be able to conduct all military activities they have been ordered to conduct, in any conditions

[854] Recruitment and selection is designed to appoint personnel in the right numbers and of the right quality to meet this requirement

[855] Personnel undergo special instruction and training which focus on specific elements derived from the deployment choices

[856] This means that in each phase of instruction and training, there must be attention for motivation, physical fitness and mental toughness (see the mental component), for knowledge of possible deployment choices, for knowledge of tactics and techniques (skills & drills), for basic skills (including physical training, physical toughness) and for specialist, function-specific skills

[857] When units or individuals are assigned to a specific mission, they might need supplementary training specifically tailored to the requirements of that mission

[858] To ensure that personnel remain available and deployable, consideration needs to be given to sustainability, motivation and mental health

[859] More and more attention is, for example, being focused on possibilities for computerised and unmanned systems to take over tasks currently performed by people

[860] Netherlands Defence Doctrine 4.2.2.2 Materiel The armed forces have a wide range of materiel at their disposal: weapon systems, vehicles, aircraft and ships, as well as associated systems such as communications equipment, optronics, fuels, ammunition, food and personal equipment

[861] These assets must be purchased, deployed, maintained, and eventually divested

[862] Much of this materiel has a specifically military character and is therefore often unique; for that reason, the armed forces have their own organisational elements responsible for these tasks

[863] Materiel under maintenance is not available for preparation or deployment

[864] The armed forces therefore normally possess several units of a particular materiel capacity

[865] This materiel can be used alternately in an ongoing cycle of readiness, deployment and maintenance, thus creating sustainability

[866] To prevent loss of capability during deployment, redundancy is often built in: anticipation of losses in numbers, extra protection of critical assets and/or duplication of critical systems such as propulsion, energy supply, computers and defence equipment

[867] In addition, logistic support is essential in realising and sustaining the operational use of materiel: the assured supply of, for example, fuel, spare parts, ammunition, food and medical supplies to the training or deployment area is often critical to the success of a military operation

[868] Standardisation/interoperability Standardisation is crucial for multinational forces to be able to operate efficiently and effectively

[869] Standardisation of assets results in particular in improved efficiency, whereas interoperability and uniformity of procedures and tactics mainly benefit effectiveness

[870] Standardisation can be achieved at three levels: compatibility, interchangeability and interoperability

[871] Compatibility is the suitability of devices, systems, processes or services for use together under specific conditions without causing unacceptable interactions or side-effects

[872] Interchangeability is the ability of one device, system, process or service to be used in place of another to achieve the same goal

[873] Interoperability is the ability to operate with other units or organisations coherently, effectively and efficiently to achieve one’s own or shared tactical, operational and/or strategic objectives

[874] Standardisation is the imposition of a particular norm or standard on the design, construction, testing and/or use of a product or on the application of a process

[875] In striving for a certain level of standardisation in the military organisation, the aim is to achieve uniformity in operational procedures and means

[876] After it had been set up, NATO recognised the importance of interoperability and the standardisation required to achieve it

[877] In NATO, standardisation is defined as “the development and implementation of concepts, doctrines, procedures and designs in order to achieve and maintain the compatibility, interchangeability or commonality which are necessary to attain the required level of interoperability”

[878] For that reason, in 1951 the Military Agency for Standardization was established, the aim of which was to supervise the standardisation of war materiel and operational and administrative procedures

[879] In the decades that followed, NATO member states agreed on a great number of standardization agreements (STANAGs) covering a multitude of subjects, such as operational and tactical doctrine and procedures, technical specifications and training requirements

[880] In 2001, the Military Agency for Standardization was merged into a new organisation called NATO Standardization Office (NSO

[881] The Netherlands supports this standardisation and is adapting its own standards (work methods and procedures, tactical and technical standards and coding) to bring them as far as possible into line with those of NATO

[882] This is helping to achieve the greatest possible interoperability with allies

[883] A simple example is the use of the English language as standard

[884] This means that concepts, doctrines and procedures are no longer translated into Dutch for use in a Dutch environment

[885] Netherlands Defence Doctrine 4.2.3 The conceptual component The conceptual component provides the coherent, intellectual basis and theoretical foundation for the deployment of military forces

[886] It is relevant for all levels of warfare; at the strategic level, it provides the intellectual background for effective decision making on the political-military dividing line

[887] The conceptual component also plays a significant role in the preservation and development of the institutional memory and experience

[888] To do so, it brings together historical experiences, developments in operational practice (through lessons learned, analyses and experiments) and an ongoing study of the strategic environment

[889] The main function of the conceptual component is to provide a conceptual framework with which military leaders can acquire an understanding of their profession and of the activities they are expected to deploy, both now and in the future

[890] The conceptual component supplies the commander with the necessary understanding of the context in which he is operating, and is the basis for creativity, ingenuity and initiative in complex situations

[891] Successful execution of military operations requires intellectually precise and clearly formulated opinions that are based on experience

[892] The intellectual activity needed to arrive at clear views is known as military thinking, and it is this intellectual process that leads to concepts for strategic, operational and tactical practice

[893] This scientific process is fed by military history, by recent experiences in conflicts and exercises, and by innovative ideas

[894] The concepts generated by this process are a response to challenges in military operations

[895] These operational concepts can form the basis of doctrine

[896] Skill in applying doctrine can only be acquired by studying and getting to grips with the conceptual component in its broadest sense

[897] Innovation The conceptual component is constantly being developed through conceptual innovation, whereby consideration is given to what changes could occur because of a changing environment, new technologies and challenges, because of a new environment or activities, methods and processes, or because existing solutions no longer work

[898] Conceptual innovation must, along with new capabilities and new methods of operation, result in the continued effectiveness and thus continued relevance of fighting power

[899] This innovation is brought about through scientific debate about military operations, by research and development and by an institutionalised process in which lessons learned are interpreted, evaluated and implemented

[900] Innovation can take place within organisations and also in a broader context

[901] The process of innovation comprises the whole of human activity aimed at renewal

[902] Innovation is more than just a technical upgrade or modernisation

[903] Innovation can also occur in, for example, command and control, management or policy

[904] The impact of a new way of using an existing work method or procedure for the introduction of a new weapons system, for example, could be as great as that of the new system itself with its much-enhanced effectiveness

[905] Innovation is wider in scope than just the conceptual component of fighting power and it also affects the physical component in particular

[906] In the ever-ongoing arms race, innovation is crucial to the armed forces

[907] In the past, many inventions originated in the military sphere (telegraph, radar, internet, etc

[908] but nowadays the military mainly follows innovations in the civil sector

[909] Innovation in the management of the armed forces is also based on knowledge from the civil sector and is financially driven

[910] As a result, its impact on the military organisation is ever more explicitly felt

[911] Netherlands Defence Doctrine 4.3 Fighting power in the operating environment Fighting power must be adapted to the operating environment in order to be effective

[912] Historically, this adaptation could be seen in the development of specialised Services: naval, land and air forces

[913] Besides these specialised Services, today’s complexity of this environment requires a more holistic approach to achieve the necessary adaptation

[914] The operating environment influences the deployment of fighting power and the decision making of a commander

[915] A good understanding of this environment is essential for fighting power to be applied in the most efficient and effective manner

[916] Models are used to gain an understanding of the complex operating environment

[917] Models provide conceptual support but only give a simplified picture of reality

[918] For the modelling of the military operating environment, domains and dimensions are used

[919] The models serve as potential conceptual frameworks in the planning of military operations

[921] The domain model Domain-based thinking is used to organise fighting power for activities in recognised domains

[922] The various domains each have their own characteristics that determine how armed forces are organised and put to use

[923] Domain-based thinking is instrumental in organising fighting power for a specific domain but can lead to a ‘single-domain’ approach; in other words, an excessive focus on individual domains

[924] The domains should be viewed as an interconnected whole: all domains are dependent on one another

[925] Eliminating or damaging a transmitter mast in the land domain may, for example, lead to communication problems in the air, at sea or in space, or may restrict access to cyberspace

[926] Besides this interdependence, synchronised military activities in different domains may enhance each other

[927] For example, a target may be identified by special operations forces on land, and the relevant information transmited via satellite

[928] That target may be eliminated by warplanes, supported by radar images from a frigate, while the enemy air defence is neutralised by a military cyberspace operation

[929] By acting in several domains, goals can be achieved in a more effective or efficient way than through a single domain

[930] This supports the case for a multi-domain approach to military operations

[931] Although the electromagnetic and acoustic spectrums make up an environment formed by physical laws, they do not actually constitute a domain

[932] Both spectrums are strongly interconnected with the five recognised domains

[933] The ability to use these spectrums – within the laws of physics – is crucial to the ability to conduct activities effectively in those domains

[934] Most militaries are divided into three Services, designed for the maritime, land and air domains

[935] Although this structure is a historical one, even today each domain has its own characteristic qualities that translate into differences in culture and types of personnel, materiel, tactics and techniques

[936] The Dutch armed forces are also set up for the three domains: sea, land and air

[937] Naval, land and air forces all have their specific capabilities and their own input in operations

[938] In addition, all the Services have an input in the cyberspace domain, the electromagnetic spectrum and the acoustic spectrum and they make use of the space domain

[939] When bringing these capabilities to bear, the effects of the Services are not limited to their own natural environment (land, sea, air, space), as many effects transcend the domain to a greater or lesser extent

[940] The underlying philosophy is that the Services support and complement each other, thereby achieving a synergetic effect, in what are better known as joint operations

[941] Characteristics of the maritime domain The world’s oceans and seas connect countries through an interdependent network of economic, financial, social and political relationships

[942] It is a fact that most of the earth is covered with water, most of the world’s population live close to the coast, and most of international trade is carried by sea, through a few vulnerable canals and international straits

[943] The maritime environment includes trade routes, chokepoints, ports, and other infrastructure important to people, such as pipelines, wind farms, platforms for the production of oil and natural gas, and overseas telecommunication cables

[944] Moreover, the sea is exploited for its economic resources, whether on the high seas, in exclusive economic zones (EEZs) or in territorial waters

[945] In short, the sea provides strategic access

[946] The maritime domain varies from the high seas to the more confined and often shallower waters of litoral regions, estuaries and the air and water columns above and below them

[947] Most human maritime activities – shipping, fishing, oil exploitation, and so on – are currently carried out in EEZs where warships and submarines have free access to conduct exercises and routine operations

[948] This means that a significant part of the world’s economic and political activities are carried out in a narrow strip of land and sea

[949] The more confined the maritime environment becomes, the greater the chance that it will be a disputed, congested, confused and restricted space

[950] Over the course of history, various agreements have been made to regulate the use of the sea and to resolve claims of countries and users

[951] Many of these agreements affect maritime operations

[952] The most important international rules are laid down in the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) and supplemented, where appropriate, by national legislation

[953] Naval forces, consisting of task forces, amphibious manoeuvre units and maritime special operations forces, are mobile and deployable worldwide, under widely varying climatic and geographical conditions, across the entire spectrum of conflict

[954] They are characterised by access, mobility, lift capacity, posture, sustained reach, resilience, leverage, versatility and availability

[955] Characteristics of the land domain The source of human existence lies in the land domain

[956] Conflicts are ultimately decided on land because that is where the continued existence and well-being of a nation, a population, a group, an ideology, etc, can be ensured

[957] There is by definition, therefore, always a land dimension inherent in a conflict

[958] Hostile land forces, both regular and irregular, operate in this domain

[959] The land domain is characterised as the ‘home base’ of the actors in the international arena

[960] It is in this domain that direct interaction occurs with the actors in a conflict

[961] It is divided into territories, separated by national borders

[962] Natural features such as mountain ranges and rivers often form natural boundaries between territories and population groups

[963] On the other hand, borders are sometimes established without taking any account of population groups, which can be a cause of conflict

[964] The land domain contains many different kinds of terrain (mountains, deserts, jungle, cities, polders, open plains), each of which affect capabilities of certain types of military operation

[965] As well as being subject to international law, as is the case in the other domains, the conduct of activities in the land domain is largely regulated by national legislation

[966] Land forces are set up to conduct operations in the land domain, adapted to geographical and climatic conditions where necessary

[967] Land forces are characterised by a multitude of small weapon systems, from the individual serviceman to advanced combat vehicles, equipped with their own sensors and communication capabilities and usually operating in larger units (battalions/brigades/divisions

[968] Provided they are supported by good logistics, land forces are able to operate in the area of operations non-stop and for prolonged periods

[1190] Manoeuvrist approach The ability to generate sufficient fighting power is no guarantee of success

[1191] An approach in which power is mainly deployed against identified weaknesses of other actors and in which it targets the mental component is known as the manoeuvrist approach

[1192] The aim of this approach is to influence other actors’ perception of reality, their behaviour and their actions

[1193] Important aspects here are momentum, tempo and mental agility, which in combination produce a shock effect and the element of surprise on other actors

[1194] The manoeuvrist approach requires a mental attitude that centres on creativity, the willingness to take risks, and perseverance

[1195] The traditional manoeuvrist approach is designed to break the will of the enemy and his willingness to carry on fighting

[1196] The emphasis is thus on his mental component and not on the destruction of his physical component; direct confrontation is avoided wherever possible

[1197] The purpose of the manoeuvrist approach is to: - gain and retain the initiative and exert constant pressure at times and places that the enemy least expects it, thus undermining his will and his willingness to continue fighting; - direct the effects of precision weapons against identified enemy weaknesses, thus breaking his cohesion; - restricting the enemy’s ability to obtain situational awareness, thus adversely affecting his understanding

[1198] A selective and physical elimination of capacities (people and means) is in keeping with the manoeuvrist approach

[1199] The manoeuvrist approach can be characterised by preventive action, by outmanoeuvring and by disrupting the enemy

[1200] The ability to identify and exploit weaknesses not only requires good intelligence, but also a high operational tempo and multiple simultaneous activities

[1201] A high operational tempo can be reached by rapid decision making, speed of execution and a fast-paced change of activities

[1202] This high tempo will allow disruption of the enemy’s decision-making cycle which will hinder his response; his will, cohesion and perception will thus be affected

[1203] Despite the emphasis on the mental component, the manoeuvrist approach in combat always involves elements of physical mobility, firepower and control of maritime areas, ground or airspace

[1204] This approach also involves the need to bind the enemy, to deny routes, spaces and areas and to preserve our own vital areas and key points

[1205] Defensive measures like these are, however, always a means to a higher end, namely the defeat of the enemy

[1206] It is not always possible to break an enemy’s will to fight

[1207] In the case of ideologically driven groups, other instruments will have to be sought to create the desired effect

[1208] This can be achieved within the philosophy of the manoeuvrist approach, by seeking the support of all actors for the operation, and also by denying external support to the enemy, as his sustainability could be partly dependent on this form of support

[1209] In this way, the enemy can be deprived of the initiative

[1210] Effective operations are determined by the approach towards all actors, not only by the way in which the enemy is approached

[1211] By extension, the ‘will of the enemy’ in the traditional manoeuvrist approach can therefore be regarded as the ‘actor’s opinion’
