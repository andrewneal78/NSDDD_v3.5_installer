[610] National Security System Until now, Vanuatu has not had a national security system, and only a few personnel specifically assigned to cover national security issues

[612] This is in line with the responsibility of any government, in safeguarding its nation and people, to have in place a national security system that is strategically-focused, well-coordinated, flexible and able to balance competing interests

[613] Such systems should be able to deal with short-term emergency situations as well as evolving and longer-term threats, and to provide strong management at both the strategic and operational level

[666] Globalisation, Interconnectivity, and Shifts in Power The global environment is increasingly dynamic and interconnected, a product of rapid technological and scientific advances

[667] This has empowered individuals, expanded our opportunities, brought economic change and helped lift many millions of people in the developing world out of poverty

[668] Increasingly however this dynamic environment is accompanied by instability, uncertainty, and heightened risk

[669] Events unfold quickly and often are more complex than before, calling for ever faster and more agile security responses to emerging challenges

[670] Our interconnected world also means that malign individuals and groups now have greater access to disruptive technologies, including sophisticated weaponry, enabling them to perpetrate large-scale violence

[671] The post-1945 international order has been characterised by an increasingly integrated world economy and the development of international rules and institutions

[672] More recent winds of change have seen increasing concerns about the effects of globalisation, and in many countries a rise in political alienation and economic nationalism

[673] Rules are being contested, and in some cases major powers are ignoring or undermining international law

[674] The post-Cold War lull in major rivalry has ended

[675] Meanwhile the centre of gravity of global economic power has continued to shift towards Asia

[676] Given the current decline in China’s economic momentum, in a relatively short time China will overtake the US in economic size, with India also rising rapidly

[677] The diffusion of power among countries will bring about huge change, with predictions that by Asia will overtake North America and Europe combined in global power, based upon GDP, population size, military spending and technological advancement

[678] But, although the dominance of the US is being challenged in the international arena, it will remain the most powerful country

[679] We are now seeing a rise in trade tensions

[680] These are compounding the sharp slow-down in cross-border trade and investment since the 2008-9 global financial crisis

[681] Should these tensions lead to a significant rise in protectionism globally, there will be a heightened risk of strategic friction, an economic slowdown, and an undermining of the rules of international commerce

[682] The world’s population, 7.7 billion in 2019, is likely to reach 9.2 billion by around 2040, as a result of increased life expectancy and continuing high birth rates in a number of developing countries

[683] In most developed countries and China, population rates will be slower and in some cases will decline, slowing their productive capacity

[684] Populations in Africa and South Asia however will continue to rise rapidly

[685] In some countries this will give rise to a heightened risk of political and social tension, driven by a growing reservoir of dissatisfied young men

[686] On the other hand, some developing countries like India have the potential, depending on their policy choices, to reap a demographic dividend which will spur economic growth

[687] Demand for both skilled and unskilled labour will drive global migration

[688] At the same time the increased flows of irregular migration and displaced persons fleeing conflicts or natural disasters - now numbering some 65 million, the largest number since the Second World War - will continue to be a source of strain both internally in many nations, and between countries

[689] Climate Change, the Environment, and Resources The world’s growing population, and particularly the rapid growth in the middle class - expected to number some 3.5 billion by 2030 in Asia alone - will strain the environment and the world’s resources

[690] By some estimates, demand for food, water and energy will grow by 35, 40 and 50 percent respectively over the next two decades

[691] There will continue to be intense pressure on forests, driven by changes in land-use, illegal logging, climate change, and the soaring demand for timber

[692] Oceans will also be under greater pressure, with further strain on global fisheries, much of which are already fished at unsustainable levels

[693] Increasing demand for critical minerals, and the need to transport them across states, will call for efficient extraction techniques and more secure routes, as well as equitable investment regimes, if tension and conflict are to be avoided

[694] Climate change, sea level rise and a greater incidence of extreme weather events will exacerbate the existing challenges

[695] Natural disasters – causing loss of life, physical destruction and disease, and in extreme cases fuelling migration, social unrest and instability – will call for more disaster relief and humanitarian assistance

[696] Vulnerable developing countries particularly will feel more pressure, due to the high cost of climate change and natural disaster adaptation and mitigation

[698] Advances in diagnostic techniques, the development of artificial organs and mind-controlled prosthetic limbs, and the development of potential cures for some cancers and other devastating illnesses, all give hope

[703] Drug-resistant tuberculosis and malaria are posing increasing challenges
