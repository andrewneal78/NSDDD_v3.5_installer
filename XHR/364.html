[116] Link Between Security and Development Uganda recognises that security and development are mutually reinforcing and interlinked

[117] Development without security is not sustainable, and security without development is not durable

[118] The linkage between the two needs to be appreciated

[119] Unresolved conflict, particularly if violent, can instantly disrupt or reverse years of development efforts, diverting resources that could be used for improving the quality of life and thereby undermining the development of the community

[120] The damage to society may not be just short term, physical or physiological but also psychological as well

[121] On the other hand, underdeveloped communities suffer insecurity and violence caused by unfair distribution of resources

[122] Underdevelopment also has its dangers

[123] An underdeveloped community may suffer insecurity and violence caused by tensions over unfair distribution and disparities or social attitudes of groups who have little invested in the community

[124] Without security and tranquillity, national issues become less relevant to the individual who focuses increasingly upon survival

[125] At its extreme, the citizens feel unprotected from both internal and external threats limiting their ability to support and contribute to national development efforts

[126] Uganda’s Core Security Interests The following are Uganda’s security interests: Uganda’s Core Security Interests a

[624] As stated in the first chapter, the challenges that face Uganda today are a manifestation of its political and military history

[625] Suffice to say, it is important to highlight some of these historical aspects that have had a direct bearing on the defence prospects of Uganda

[626] At the onset of colonialism, Uganda was not a single political entity as is the case today, however, the people of Uganda were quite homogeneous or, at least, linked historically

[627] There are four linguistic groups in Uganda: i.e the Bantu, the Nilotics, the Nilo-Hamatic and the Sudanic

[628] There is evidence that the whole of western Uganda and much of Buganda were either governed together up to 1600 AD or were closely linked somehow during the dynasty of the Bachwezi, and during Bunyoro Kitara empire

[629] There was clear interaction between the Bantu of Central and Western Uganda with the Acholi and Alur of Northern Uganda

[630] It is a historical fact that the Karimojong and Iteso of Eastern Uganda had a longer association with the southern and northern tribes of Uganda long before the onset of colonialism

[631] Therefore, the notion that the peoples of Uganda are so different, that they had no homogeneities or linkages until the British came and put together an “artificial” unit known as Uganda, is untrue

[632] By 1800 AD, the kinship groups of the Western, Eastern and Central Uganda with their new Luo relatives together with the Karimojong, Iteso and the Bagwere of Eastern Uganda were not governed together although they were linked

[633] Indeed the successor kingdoms to the Bacwezi dynasty namely Buganda, Bunyoro and Nkore, were always in contact, sometimes allying and at others fighting among themselves

[634] These same kingdoms also traded together

[635] They were also linked with the chiefdoms of Busoga and Acholi and to a great extent the kingdoms of Rwanda, Karagwe and the chiefdoms of Bukoba in Tanzania

[636] Nevertheless, they remained a constellation of kinship states

[637] That is why (albeit belatedly) some of our kings (Mwanga and Kabalega), supported by the Langis came together to fight British colonialism

[638] The political fragmentation and animosity experienced among these kinship groups, is, mainly, a result of colonial manipulation and ideological confusion of the African leaders that assumed leadership at independence

[639] All this was made worse by a pre-industrial economy that suffers from lack of horizontal linkages

[640] The arising tensions must be contained in order to allow transformation to take place

[641] Therefore, when identifying threats to our security and designing a defence response, Uganda’s historical background must be taken into account
