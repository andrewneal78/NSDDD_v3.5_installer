[657] Total Defence Workforce

[658] As a small but heavily deployed force, the NZDF must be optimally configured if it is to deliver on government policy

[659] This especially means improving the ratio between front line personnel and those in the middle and back

[660] It also means making sure that it has the right balance between full-time and part-time military personnel, and between uniformed personnel and civilians

[661] A major outcome of this White Paper will be the adoption of a ‘Total Defence Workforce’ approach, as a new way to manage personnel flows within the NZDF and improve organisational performance

[662] The core principle of Total Defence Workforce is that any person in the NZDF (whether uniformed or civilian, full-time or part-time) will be able to apply for any non-operational position, with the essential determinants for selection being competencies and availability

[663] The aim will be to ensure the best match of position to skills, no matter from where those skills come

[664] This approach will enable a greater number of roles in the corporate parts of the NZDF to be filled by civilians, who can often provide specialist organisational skills at a lower cost

[665] It will free up uniformed personnel to fill more front line military positions

[666] It may allow for more stability in the wider NZDF workforce, by providing all staff with a greater range of career opportunities

[667] And it will underpin the modernisation of the Reserve Force, focusing more on the skills required than on numbers (while recognising the value of maintaining a Reserve Force presence in regional New Zealand

[668] Total Defence Workforce will also enable and encourage former Regular Force, Reserve Force, and civilian personnel to return to service in the future

[669] It will be made much easier to exit and re-engage

[670] Career patterns People increasingly want variety in their careers, either by tackling different roles within an existing organisation or by looking for new challenges altogether

[1019] They will not diminish their command responsibilities, nor will they dilute the unique character of each Service

[1020] They will, however, make it clear both within the NZDF and to those outside that the CDF has overall accountability for (and so the ultimate authority over) the management and operation of the entire NZDF

[1021] The Commander Joint Forces New Zealand (COMJFNZ) will be appointed by the CDF in consultation with the Minister of Defence

[1022] This different set of arrangements recognises not only the operational nature of the COMJFNZ role but also that COMJFNZ exercises his operational authority essentially as an agent of the CDF

[1023] In terms of broadening the expertise of the Executive Leadership Team, a new civilian post of Chief Operating Officer (COO) will be established

[1024] The person appointed to this position will be the CDF’s deputy in managing the NZDF as an organisation, and will drive the reforms needed to redistribute existing resources to front line capability

[1025] Working to the CDF, the COO will ensure that such reforms are planned, executed, and monitored comprehensively and with vigour

[1026] The COO position will sit alongside and be equal in status to the VCDF, who will continue to be primarily responsible for military functions

[1027] The CDF will appoint the COO, in consultation with the State Services Commissioner and following endorsement by Cabinet

[1028] As well as strengthening the membership of the Executive Leadership Team in this way, steps will be taken to provide the CDF with improved performance management data (including on the reform programme) and better quality financial information, including a capital investment plan

[1029] Accountability of the CDF 9.14 The State Services Commissioner manages the process of appointing public service chief executives, including the Secretary of Defence (the Secretary), and takes the lead in assessing and managing their performance

[1030] These arrangements do not currently apply to the CDF

[1031] Although the State Services Commissioner assists the Minister in the process of appointing the CDF, and will continue to do so, the Commissioner in future will also assist the Minister in the evaluation and management of the CDF’s performance

[1032] Performance expectations relating to military leadership and the effective management of the NZDF will be set for the CDF, including achieving the resource redistribution targets in this White Paper

[1033] The CDF will be accountable for achieving these expectations

[1034] Working together

[1035] The functions and accountabilities of the CDF and the Secretary are set out in current legislation, most notably the Defence Act 1990, the State Sector Act 1988, and the Public Finance Act

[1036] The NZDF and the Ministry are each charged with undertaking specific roles and tasks

[1037] If the best results are to be achieved, it is essential that the Secretary, as the Government’s principal civilian adviser on defence matters, and the CDF, as the Government’s principal military adviser, work closely together

[1038] The Defence Act requires that the Secretary consult with the Chief of Defence Force in the formulation of advice on defence policy

[1039] This requirement acknowledges the different areas of expertise that must be brought together if the Government is to receive complete and coherent advice

[1040] The Ministry’s resources for this task will be strengthened to ensure that it is able to offer a comprehensive view of the links between policy on the one hand and capability and funding on the other

[1041] This strengthening will focus on depth rather than breadth, with an increase in the number of experienced staff

[1042] Reciprocal access to information between the Ministry and the NZDF will be mandated, as a way to ensure that Defence policy advice is a coherent and seamless reflection of all the relevant material which is available within the two organisations

[1043] The role and capability of the Evaluation Division of the Ministry will also be strengthened

[1044] The Defence Act tasks the Secretary to procure, replace, or repair ships, vehicles, aircraft, and equipment for use by the NZDF, where they have major significance to military capability

[1045] Although the Act gives this responsibility solely to the Secretary, in practice the Secretary and CDF work closely together through all phases of a capability’s life cycle

[1046] Indeed, they must work together

[1047] Not only does each life cycle phase (see following diagram require both policy and military expertise, but the overall success of any major procurement demands that the Secretary and the CDF retain oversight of the process as a whole

[1048] Defence Capabilities - Lifecycle Phases Capability Definition and selection ► Strategic Policy ► Acquisition ► Introduction Into Service ►

[1049] In Service ► Disposal In a very real sense, then, these phases are not distinct and separate but rather part of a seamless and dynamic process of management which covers the whole operational life of a capability from policy specification, through to acquisition and operational use, and then eventual disposal

[1050] And just as the end-user (the NZDF) has an interest in every step in this process, so the Ministry must ensure that decisions taken throughout the process are informed by policy considerations

[1051] Existing arrangements 9.26 The existing procurement process can work well — New Zealand has for the most part bought useable equipment at a good price

[1052] But there are also examples of problems

[1053] The 2008 reports from the Office of the Auditor-General and from John Coles (on HMNZS Canterbury) identified shortcomings in the management of some procurement projects

[1054] For this reason, the Secretary, as part of his Defence Assessment, commissioned Michael Wintringham and Aurecon Ltd. to examine aspects of Defence organisational arrangements and the procurement process respectively, and to recommend ways to improve them

[1055] Procurement was also considered by the VfM team

[1056] The reviews all highlighted what was seen as disjointed and fragmented management across the capability life cycle, with confusion particularly acute at the transition point between the policy and acquisition phases (led by the Ministry) and the in-service use and disposal phases (led by the NZDF

[1057] Whole-of-life capability management In looking to remedy these problems, the Government recognises that the NZDF and the Ministry must work together across all phases of the capability life cycle

[1058] The continuous and effective co-operation which is needed must be driven from the top of the two organisations, and must be reflected in the formal accountabilities of the Secretary and CDF

[1059] These accountabilities will exist not only for the individual phases of a capability project but also for the management of the capability life cycle as a whole

[1060] The Ministry will ordinarily lead and be accountable for the policy, capability development, and acquisition phases, and the NZDF will ordinarily lead and be accountable for the introduction into service, in-service, and disposal phases, unless the Minister directs otherwise

[1061] Each phase will be executed through a clearly identified (and accountable) project director or manager, operating within a co-operative framework

[1062] In many instances this will be given effect through the creation of joint staffs, with NZDF and Ministry personnel working together as part of a formally constituted team

[1063] A ‘capability sponsor’ will also be formally identified for each major acquisition

[1064] This will usually be a Service Chief, but could for example also be the Commander Joint Forces New Zealand or the Chief Information Officer

[1065] The ‘capability sponsor’ will ensure that the requirements of the chief executives in each phase of the capability life cycle are met, including by providing all necessary personnel

[1066] Notwithstanding these working level arrangements, the accountability for each individual phase of the capability life cycle will rest with the relevant chief executive

[1067] Recognising that the overall success for managing the life cycle of any major acquisition requires close co-operation between the two organisations, the Minister of Defence (and the Cabinet) will expect any problem which threatens the overall success of that life cycle to be identified by either or both of the two chief executives and addressed by them both, acting collaboratively

[1068] Such collaboration will in future also be assisted by advice from outside experts who will be appointed to Defence’s internal capability management body

[1069] This approach is a less radical option than some alternatives, such as reuniting the Ministry and the NZDF within a ‘diarchy’ (the model which prevails in Australia, Canada, and the United Kingdom

[1070] Instead, it retains the benefits of having clear and separate accountabilities for each of the two chief executives while encouraging collaborative activity where that matters

[1071] Defence Advisory Board The Defence Review profited from the skills and insights provided by experts outside of Defence as well as within

[1072] This will be made a standing arrangement

[1073] External contributions to Defence’s business will be provided through a new independent Defence Advisory Board to be appointed by the Minister of Defence

[1074] The Board will provide the Minister with independent advice

[1075] Conclusion The NZDF needs the right equipment, people, and infrastructure if it is to deliver the policy outcomes identified in this White Paper

[1076] It also needs a modern, focused, and responsive organisation to support that delivery

[1077] The current structures have a number of strengths

[1078] But they can also be improved

[1079] CDF lacks some of the authority and accountability of other public service chief executives

[1080] The split between the Ministry and the NZDF can at times be a barrier to effectiveness

[1081] And the focus of the NZDF on the effective delivery of military outputs in a demanding environment has seen less attention paid to organisational matters

[1082] These issues will be addressed

[1083] The CDF will be given more authority and more clearly defined accountability as chief executive

[1084] The new COO will drive the reform programme

[1085] And corporate management skills throughout the organisation will be strengthened

[1086] The changes in organisational arrangements will be backed up by strengthening internal monitoring mechanisms and by creating an independent Defence Advisory Board

[1087] These initiatives, taken cumulatively, will be a step-change in how Defence is managed, and will facilitate the reforms needed to ensure that the NZDF is not only modern, professional, and effective in the field but also in its organisation

[1088] Indeed, strengthening the organisation in this way will deliver the NZDF the operational capabilities it needs — now and into the future
