[4] The Long-Term Defence Development Plan 2009 – 2018 (Plan 2018) summary Dear reader, On 22 January 2009, the Government of the Republic of Estonia approved the “Estonian Long Term Defence Deve-lopment Plan 2009 – 2018” (hereafter Plan 2018), which establishes the main development areas of the Estonian Defence Forces for the next decade

[5] Below you will find an overview of how the Plan 2018 was drafted, what will be the main capability development areas and which investments will follow

[6] To reach the goals of Estonia’s national defence, our defence policy is based on principles as agreed by the Parliament in June 2004: indivisibility of security, solidarity and cooperation, credible military deterrence, unity of national defence and total defence, collective security, support to development of European Union capabilities in the framework of European Security and Defence Policy and taking part in ESDP; and democratic control of Estonian Defence Forces

[7] Estonian military defence is part of integrated national defence and is based on two equally important pillars – developing essential capabilities for initial self-defence and collective defence with Estonia’s NATO mem-bership as its cornerstone

[8] These two together will create adequate deter-rence to prevent any potential aggression

[9] The defence of Estonian territory against any large-scale attack will be conducted as a NATO collective defence operation together with Allies

[26] How was the Plan 2018 drafted

[27] The Plan 2018 was drafted in close cooperation between the experts of the Ministry of Defence, Headquarters of the Estonian Defence Forces, Headquarters of the Defence League, and the Information Board

[28] The first step in drafting of the Plan 2018 included compilation of potential threat scenarios

[29] The scenarios were based on the newest strategic threat assessment and were done in cooperation of experts from the Information Board, the Security Police Board, the Defence Forces Intelligence Department and the Ministry of Defence

[30] The scenarios addressed potential threats to Estonia in the near future requiring the use of the defence forces and the Defence League

[31] We have analysed the full spectrum of potential threats to Estonia

[32] The strategic threat assessment and threat scenarios are confidential due to obvious reasons, but in general it can be said that in the current security environment there is no threat of a large-scale military attack to Estonia as a member of NATO

[33] Still, the threat scenarios illustrate that Estonia must be ready to defend herself in case of external pressure and show of force, for which it is necessary to create a credible and functioning deterrence together with Allies

[34] In general terms, the threats to nation’s security remain as described in the National Security Strategy

[37] Based on these illustrative scenarios operational analysis was conducted

[38] This identified military capabilities required for national defence

[39] In the following step capabilities required were compared with capabilities existing – this identified capability gaps

[40] The assessment of current and required military capabilities was carried out to establish an overall picture of the current situation and desired end state

[41] Capabilities required were then prioritized after weighted risk analysis

[42] The main logic behind the prioritization was wide usability of capabilities while minimizing the risk to national defence, taking also into account possible resource implications – hence focusing on usable, flexible and interoperable capabilities

[43] In the following programming stage we determined the level to which any capability should be developed and their resource implications: in terms of finances, personnel and time

[44] The programming resulted in three categories of capabilities

[45] Capabilities that are necessary for national defence, but are too expensive for Estonia and can be achieved only in cooperation with NATO and Allies

[46] In the case of Estonia, such capabilities are, for example, fighter aircraft and attack helicopters

[47] Strategic Airlift is another example of capabilities in this category, and to ensure this, Estonia has joined NATO’s Strategic Airlift Capability (SAC) project

[48] Capabilities that are necessary for national defence, but are unreachable within the next 10 years

[49] Such capabilities identified during programming are, for example, multiple launch rocket systems with a range of up to 70 kilometres and surface-to-surface missile systems required for destroying surface naval targets

[50] The development of such capabilities could be considered after 2018 or in case some other projects in development will have significantly lower resource implications than originally planned

[51] Capabilities that are necessary for national defence and the development of which can be launched within the next 10 years

[52] Those capabilities form the basis of the Plan

[53] All in all, such a planning model guarantees that the Plan 2018 corresponds to the current security environment, is drafted considering both military and civil expertise, and is consistent with existing resources

[55] Communication and intelligence systems will improve One of the main priorities of the Plan 2018 is to develop the command and control, intelligence, surveillance and communications systems of the EDF

[56] In modern warfare, uninterrupted, encrypted and jam proof commu-nications play an especially important role

[57] Without proper communications it is impossible to conduct effective command and control of military units

[58] In order to develop this capability, large scale investments are planned for the next 10 years for all levels of command, starting with a single soldier on battlefield equipped with modern systems all the way to leadership of defence equipped with modern command- and communica-tions assets

[59] The development of modern command, control and communications capabilities is not only important for effectively commanding our own forces, it must also be interoperable with those of our Allies

[60] In a way we are talking of “invisible” capabilities for average citizen, capabilities that cannot be seen on parades – but at the same time they require huge financial investments that cannot be substituted with anything less

[61] The development of intelligence and surveillance capabilities will pro-vide the senior military-political leadership with situational awareness on events taking place on Estonian territory, in airspace, and territorial waters

[62] These capabilities should guarantee adequate advance warning in case of potential threats and will provide military-political leadership with enough time to make respective decisions and necessary preparations

[63] In order to develop these capabilities, additional medium-range radar systems and various types of sensors will be acquired

[64] Figuratively speaking, the intelligence and surveillance systems will pro-vide the EDF with eyes and ears to collect important information in real-time

[65] Command, control and communications systems will ensure that decisions are based on optimal information and will reach all levels of command in real-time

[147] The Long-Term Defence Development Plan 2009 – 2018 (Plan 2018) summary Development of defence planning in Estonia can be characterised as the search for a perfect plan that would solve all questions rela-ted to national defence

[148] It is clear that – against a background of limited resources and changing security environment – the requirement is rather for an organised process offering modern solutions: “evolution instead of revolution”

[149] Today we have achieved a clear vision of what a suitable defence planning model for Estonia should look like; one that considers the specificities of Estonia as a small state, but also considers the requi-rements for interoperability with Allies in defence of our common values

[150] That being said, the Plan is not a document giving answers to all the challenges rising today or in the foreseeable future

[151] It is rather a tool translating today’s strategic security and defence policy guidelines into military capabilities and respective develop-ment programmes within specified resources and timeframes

[152] The policy guidance for the Plan 2018 was derived from the following documents: National Security Concept, National Military Strategy, NATO’s Strategic Concept, NATO’s Comprehensive Political Guidance and The European Security Strategy

[153] The foundations of Estonia’s national defence rely on two strategic pillars: NATO membership and initial self-defence capability

[154] These pillars together create a credible deterrence

[155] Estonia’s military defence will be conducted as a collective defence operation of NATO, as prescribed in Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty

[156] As the first step in developing the Plan 2018, scenarios requiring the use of Estonian Defence Forces (EDF) and the Defence League, based on the National Military Strategy and strategic threat assess-ment, were drafted

[157] These scenarios covered the full spectrum of operations

[158] Based on scenarios, operational analysis was conducted, and further detailed planning situations were developed

[159] This – with consideration to established assumptions, exceptions and limitations – identified minimum military capabilities required to fulfil previously established tasks

[160] The comparison of existing and required capabilities identified capability gaps, or shortfalls

[161] These were prioritised based on risk analysis of threat assessments and the usability of capabilities in planning situations

[162] The subsequent programming stage established recommended development levels and resource requirements for these capabilities

[163] As a result of programming process, three categories of capabilities were identified: capabilities militarily valid, but disproportionately costly for Estonia – these could be developed in cooperation with NATO and/or Allies; capabilities militarily valid, but not feasible to be developed within the planning cycle; capabilities militarily valid and feasible within the 10-year planning period
