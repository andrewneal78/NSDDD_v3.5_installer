[285] CAPABILITIES-BASED APPROACH Capabilities-based planning focuses more on how adversaries may challenge us than on whom those adversaries might be or where we might face them

[287] It recognizes the limits of intelligence and the impossibility of predicting complex events with precision

[288] Our planning aims to link capabilities to joint operating concepts across a broad range of scenarios

[289] The Department is adopting a new approach for planning to implement our strategy

[290] The defense strategy will drive this top-down, competitive process

[291] Operating within fiscal constraints, our new approach enables the Secretary of Defense and Joint Force Commanders to balance risk across traditional, irregular, disruptive, and catastrophic challenges

[292] We will operationalize this strategy to address the spectrum of strategic challenges by setting priorities among competing capabilities

[293] Effectively managing defense risks is central to executing the National Defense Strategy

[294] The 2001 QDR is the Department's vehicle for risk assessment

[296] It allows the Secretary of Defense to assess the tradeoffs among objectives and resource constraints

[297] The risk framework comprises: operational risk,future challenges risk,force management risk, and institutional risk: • Operational risks are those associated with the current force executing the strategy successfully within acceptable human, material, financial, and strategic costs

[298] Future challenges risks are those associated with the Department's capacity to execute future missions successfully against an array of prospective future challengers. Force management risks are those associated with managing military forces fulfilling the missions described in this National Defense Strategy

[300] Institutional risks are those associated with the capacity of new command, management, and business practices

[301] We assess the likelihood of a variety of problems—most notably, failure or prohibitive costs in pursuit of strategic, operational, or management objectives

[302] This approach recognizes that some objectives, though desirable, may not be attainable, while others, though attainable, may not be worth the costs

[303] Choices in one area affect choices in others

[304] The Department will make deliberate choices within and across each broad category and will maintain a balance among them driven by this National Defense Strategy

[305] We will consider the full range of risks associated with resources and operations and manage explicit tradeoffs across the Department

[328] Secure bases of operation make possible our political and military freedom of action, reassure the nation and its partners, and enable the timely generation and deployment of military forces worldwide

[350] PROJECTING AND SUSTAINING FORCES IN DISTANT ANTI-ACCESS ENVIRONMENTS Our role in the world depends on effectively projecting and sustaining our forces in distant environments where adversaries may seek to deny us access

[351] Our capacity to project power depends on our defense posture and deployment flexibility at home and overseas, on the security of our bases, and on our access to the strategic commons

[352] Adversaries could employ advanced and legacy military capabilities and methods to deny us access

[353] Ultimately, they may combine their most advanced military capabilities with future technologies to threaten our capacity to project power

[354] Other opponents may employ less sophisticated but effective means either to deny access to us or intimidate others to do so

[355] Their options are numerous, including the innovative employment of legacy capabilities and indirect threats intended to impose unacceptable costs on friendly governments

[356] We will project and sustain our forces in distant anti-access environments

[357] Adversaries who threaten the United States and its interests require secure bases

[358] They will use great distance or the sanctuary created by ungoverned territory to their advantage

[359] The more we hold adversaries' critical bases of operation at risk, the more likely we are to limit their strategic options

[360] A key goal is developing the capability to surge military forces rapidly from strategic distances to deny adversaries sanctuary

[361] In some cases, this will involve discrete Special Operations Forces (SOF) or precision attacks on targets deep inside enemy territory

[362] In others, sustained joint or combined combat operations will be necessary, requiring the comprehensive defeat of significant state and non-state opponents operating in and from enemy territory or an ungoverned area

[363] To deny sanctuary requires a number of capabilities, including: persistent surveillance and precision strike; operational maneuver from strategic distances; sustained joint combat operations in and from austere locations, at significant operational depths; and stability operations to assist in the establishment of effective and responsible control over ungoverned territory

[364] We will deny our enemies sanctuary by conducting effective military activities and operations in and from austere geographic locations and at varying operational depths

[365] CONDUCTING NETWORK-CENTRIC OPERATIONS

[366] The foundation of our operations proceeds from a simple proposition: the whole of an integrated and networked force is far more capable than the sum of its parts

[367] Continuing advances in information and communications technologies hold promise for networking highly distributed joint and combined forces

[368] Network-centric operational capability is achieved by linking compatible information systems with usable data

[369] The functions of sensing, decision-making, and acting-which often in the past were built into a single platform—now can work closely even if they are geographically distributed across the battle space

[370] Bringing decisive capabilities to bear increasingly will rely on our capacity to harness and protect advantages in the realm of information

[371] Networking our forces will provide the foundation for doing so

[372] Operations in the war on terrorism have demonstrated the advantages of timely and accurate information, while at the same time reinforcing the need for even greater joint, interoperable command, control, communications, computers, intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (C4ISR

[373] Beyond battlefield applications, a network-centric force can increase efficiency and effectiveness across defense operations, intelligence functions, and business processes by giving all users access to the latest, most relevant, most accurate information

[374] It also enables "reach-back" by more effectively employing people and capabilities without deploying them forward

[375] Transforming to a network-centric force requires fundamental changes in processes, policy, and culture

[376] Change in these areas will provide the necessary speed, accuracy, and quality of decision-making critical to future success

[377] We will conduct network-centric operations with compatible information and communications systems, usable data, and flexible operational constructs

[425] Defend the homeland

[426] Our most important contribution to the security of the U.S. homeland is our capacity to identify, disrupt, and defeat threats early and at a safe distance, as far from the United States and its partners as possible

[427] Our ability to identify and defeat threats abroad-before they can strike-while making critical contributions to the direct defense of our territory and population is the sine qua non of our nation's security

[428] Operate in and from four forward regions

[429] Our military presence abroad comprises tailored and increasingly rotational forces operating in and from four forward regions -Europe, Northeast Asia, the East Asian Littoral, and the Middle East-Southwest Asia

[430] Complemented by our capabilities for prompt global action, our forces overseas help assure partners, dissuade military competition, and deter aggression and coercion

[431] Our forward deterrence capabilities, in particular, are adaptable forces able to respond rapidly to emerging crises and control escalation on our terms

[433] Our military presence in the four regions does not constrain our capacity to undertake military missions worldwide, nor does it delimit our global interests

[434] For example, we remain steadfast in our commitment to the security of the Americas, yet we require a very small military presence in Central and South America

[435] Our current military presence abroad recognizes that significant U.S. interests and the bulk of our forward military presence are concentrated in the four regions, even as our forces are positioned to undertake military operations worldwide

[437] We cannot be certain in advance about the location and specific dimensions of future conflicts

[439] It is capable of surging forces into two separate theaters to "swiftly defeat" adversaries in military campaigns that overlap in time

[443] Conduct lesser contingencies

[444] Our global interests require our armed forces to undertake a limited number of lesser contingency operations, perhaps for extended periods of time

[445] Lesser contingencies include smaller-scale combat operations such as strikes and raids; peace operations; humanitarian missions; and non-combatant evacuations

[446] Because these contingencies place burdens on the same types of forces needed for more demanding military campaigns, the Department closely monitors the degree and nature of involvement in lesser contingencies to properly balance force management and operational risks

[447] GLOBAL DEFENSE POSTURE

[448] To better meet new strategic circumstances, we are transforming our network of alliances and partnerships, our military capabilities, and our global defense posture

[449] Our security is inextricably linked to that of our partners

[450] The forward posture of U.S. forces and our demonstrated ability to bring forces to bear in a crisis are among the most tangible signals of our commitment to the security of our international partners

[451] Through the 1990s, U. S. forces remained concentrated in Cold War locations primarily in Western Europe and Northeast Asia

[452] In the Cold War we positioned our forces to fight where they were stationed

[453] Today, we no longer expect our forces to fight in place

[454] Rather, operational experience since 1990 indicates we will surge forces from a global posture to respond to crises

[455] This recognition, combined with rapid advances in technology, new concepts of operation, and operational lessons learned, is driving a comprehensive realignment of U.S. global defense posture

[456] The President stated, "A fully transformed and strengthened overseas force posture will underscore the commitment of the United States to effective collective action in the common cause of peace and liberty

[457] Force posture changes will strengthen our ability to meet our security commitments and contend with new challenges more effectively

[458] As we transform our posture, we are guided by the following goals: - Expanding allied roles and building new security partnerships; - Developing greater flexibility to contend with uncertainty by emphasizing agility and by not overly concentrating military forces in a few locations; - Focusing within and across regions by complementing tailored regional military presence and activities with capabilities for prompt global military action; - Developing rapidly deployable capabilities by planning and operating from the premise that forces will not likely fight in place; and, - Focusing on capabilities, not numbers, by reinforcing the premise that the United States does not need specific numbers of platforms or personnel in administrative regions to be able to execute its security commitments effectively

[459] Key changes to global defense posture

[460] Key changes in global defense posture lie in five interrelated areas: relationships, activities, facilities, legal arrangements, and global sourcing and surge

[461] Our ability to cooperate with others in the world depends on having a harmony of views on the challenges that confront us and our strategy for meeting those challenges

[462] Strengthening defense relationships at all levels helps build such harmony

[463] Changes in global posture seek both to strengthen our relationships with partners around the world and to help cultivate new relationships founded on common security interests

[464] We are transforming many of our alliances to contend with our new circumstances

[465] Command structures are another important part of our relationships and are being tailored to address our new political and operational needs

[466] We also will lower the operational vulnerability of our forces and reduce local social and political friction with host populations

[467] Our posture also includes the many military activities in which we engage around the world

[468] This means not only our physical presence in key regions, but also our training, exercises, and operations

[469] They involve small units working together in a wide range of capacities, major formations conducting elaborate exercises to achieve proficiency in joint and combined operations, and the "nuts and bolts" of providing support to ongoing operations

[470] They also involve the force protection that we and our allies provide to each other. Facilities

[471] A network of forward facilities and capabilities, mainly in four critical regions, provides the United States with an unmatched ability to act globally

[472] However, the threat posed by catastrophic challenges and the risks of surprise place an even higher premium on the ability to take rapid military action

[473] To strengthen our capability for prompt global action and our flexibility to employ military forces where needed, we require the capacity to move swiftly into and through strategic pivot points and remote locations

[474] The new global posture-using major operating bases (MOB), forward operating sites (FOS), and a diverse array of more austere cooperative security locations (CSL) -will support such needs

[475] In addition, our prepositioned equipment and stocks overseas will be better configured and positioned for global employment

[476] We will make better use of “reach-back” capabilities for those functions that can be accomplished without deploying forward

[477] MOBs are permanent bases with resident forces and robust infrastructure

[478] They are intended to support training, security cooperation, and the deployment and employment of military forces for operations

[479] The more austere facilities—FOSs and CSLs—are focal points for combined training and will expand and contract as needed to support military operations

[480] FOSs are scalable, "warm" facilities intended for rotational use by operational forces

[481] They often house pre-positioned equipment and a modest, permanent support presence

[482] FOSs are able to support a range of military activities on short notice

[483] CSLs are intended for contingency access, logistical support, and rotational use by operational forces

[484] CSLs generally will have little or no permanent U. S. personnel assigned

[485] In addition to these, joint sea-basing too holds promise for the broader transformation of our overseas military posture

[486] Increasing the flexibility and support provided by prepositioned equipment and materiel is another important aspect of our facilities infrastructure

[487] A decade of operational experience indicates that a new, more innovative approach to prepositioned equipment and stocks is needed

[488] Support materiel and combat capabilities should be positioned in critical regions and along key transportation routes to enable worldwide deployment

[489] Prepositioned capabilities afloat are especially valuable

[490] In addition, single-service prepositioned capabilities will no longer suffice

[491] As in all other aspects of transformation, prepositioning must be increasingly joint in character

[492] The new posture will be enabled by "reach-back" capabilities-support capabilities that are available remotely rather than in forward theaters

[493] For example, intelligence support, including battle damage assessment, can be provided from outside the theater of operations

[494] Leveraging reach-back capabilities reduces our footprint abroad and strengthens our military effectiveness

[495] We also seek to increase the involvement of our partners in reach-back functions

[496] Many of the current legal arrangements that govern overseas posture date from an earlier era

[497] Today, challenges are more diverse and complex, our prospective contingencies are more widely dispersed, and our international partners are more numerous

[498] International agreements relevant to our posture must reflect these circumstances and support greater operational flexibility

[499] They must help, not hinder, the rapid deployment and employment of U. S. and coalition forces worldwide in a crisis

[500] Consistent with our partners' sovereign considerations, we will seek new legal arrangements that maximize our freedom to: Deploy our forces as needed; Conduct essential training with partners in the host nation; and Support deployed forces around the world

[501] Finally, legal arrangements should encourage responsibility-sharing between us and our partners, and provide legal protections for our personnel through Status of Forces Agreements and protections against transfers of U. S. personnel to the International Criminal Court

[502] Global sourcing and surge

[503] Our military needs to be managed in a way that will allow us to deploy a greater percentage or our force where and when it is needed, anywhere in the world

[504] Thus, the Department is transitioning to a global force management process

[505] This will allow us to source our force needs from a global, rather than regional, perspective and to surge capabilities when needed into crisis theaters from disparate locations worldwide

[506] Our global presence will be managed dynamically, ensuring that our joint capabilities are employed to the greatest effect

[507] Under this concept, Combatant Commanders no longer "own" forces in their theaters

[508] Forces are allocated to them as needed sourced from anywhere in the world

[509] This allows for greater flexibility to meet rapidly changing operational circumstances

[510] A prominent consideration in our global posture changes is to move our most rapidly deployable capabilities forward

[511] For example, heavy forces will return to the United States, to be replaced in large part by more expeditionary capabilities such as airborne forces and Stryker brigades

[512] As a result, our immediate response times should be greatly improved
