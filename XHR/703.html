Section 1: Concept for comprehensive security Introduction The Security Strategy for Society describes the concept for comprehensive security, in which the vital functions of society are taken care of in collaboration between the authorities, business community, organisations and citizens

[83] Comprehensive security forms the foundation of resilience in Finnish society

[84] The objective of the Security Strategy for Society, adopted as a Government resolution, is to provide all actors with a common framework for implementing comprehensive security from their perspectives

[85] Section one of this strategy describes the vital functions of society, the assessment of related risks, the strategic tasks to secure the functions, as well as the roles of various actors

[86] The strategic tasks assigned for the administrative branches are described in Section two of this strategy

[87] The principles of comprehensive security have been updated in this Security Strategy for Society for the fifth time

[88] The first, Strategy for Securing Vital Functions for Society, was published in

[89] It described threat scenarios for societal preparedness, the strategic tasks of ministries and the fundamentals of crisis management

[90] The 2006 strategy focused on the management of disruptions and introduced an extensive matrix to support preparedness planning

[91] In 2010, the strategy was renamed as the Security Strategy for Society

[92] This change reflected the overall development in society as a result of which security operations extended to new areas, services and functions

[93] Non-governmental organisations (NGOs) were also incorporated in the preparedness measures

[94] The 2017 strategy was a practical interpretation of shared and general principles of preparedness

[95] The strategy depicted comprehensive security primarily as a collaboration model, in which actors share and analyse security-related information and develop plans, conduct exercises and work in collaboration

[96] This marked a shift from a state-centric approach to a whole-of-society model

[97] Similarly, in the current strategy comprehensive security concerns society as a whole

[98] Alongside preparedness, a new theme in the strategy is response to disruptions and crises

[99] Instead of focusing on the vital functions and security actors, the strategy emphasises action

[100] The changed security environment, Finland’s membership of the North Atlantic Treaty Alliance (NATO) and the lessons learnt from crises support this development

[101] The prolonged COVID-19 pandemic, which required declaring a state of emergency, and other disruptions in our society such as damage to critical infrastructure, instrumentalized migration and energy issues have demonstrated that Finland’s concept for comprehensive security as such provides a solid foundation for resilience

[102] However, these disruptions have also highlighted areas for improvement in our system

[103] The lessons learnt from Russia's invasion of Ukraine are also being incorporated in the development work

[104] The government has launched several projects to address the observations

[105] Administrative changes, such as the establishment of the wellbeing services counties, also necessitate renewal of the strategy’s particulars

[106] The aim has been to write the strategy to stand the test of time

[107] Describing local-level actors, in particular, in a detailed manner is difficult due to several ongoing reforms

[108] In such cases, the language chosen is intentionally more general

[109] The strategy does not define new tasks or responsibilities for actors but describes the current state to reinforce a consistent and unified foundation for action

[110] The Finnish concept for comprehensive security has also raised extensive interest internationally as a result of Finland’s NATO membership and the evolving security situation

[111] Finland itself also promotes the preparedness ideology as part of European operating models

[112] In the strategy, the concept is presented in a manner that is internationally understandable and does not require the reader to have a detailed knowledge of Finnish legislation or societal structures

[113] The importance of the European Union (EU) and NATO to the security of our society is also expanded upon

[114] The writing of the strategy was harmonised with the preparation of the Government Report on Finnish Foreign and Security Policy and the Government’s Defence Report

[115] In addition, consistency with other documents which are central to comprehensive security, such as the Government Report on Internal Security and the National Risk Assessment, has been ensured

[116] Based on the Government Programme of Prime Minister Petteri Orpo, several security-related projects are being prepared simultaneously

[117] These projects are related to the development of the operating model for government security management and the national implementation of the EU’s Critical Entities Resilience (CER) directive, among others

[118] It is important that they encompass security consistently

[119] The European standard on crisis management (EN ISO 22361:2022) and the NATO Crisis Response System manual have been applied in the formation of the new strategy

[120] Where applicable, the used terminology is based on the Vocabulary of Comprehensive Security, which will also include the new key concepts of security for society in the future

[121] An essential aspect of the execution of the strategy is monitoring its implementation

[122] In Finland, comprehensive security reporting is carried out through a Security Report for Society that is annually submitted to the state leadership, as well as other assessments and reviews published regularly

[123] These will ensure that different actors stay informed of the state of security in Finnish society

[124] Concept for comprehensive security in brief Comprehensive security is the foundation of resilience in Finnish society

[125] In the concept for comprehensive security, the vital functions of society are taken care of in collaboration between the authorities, business community, organisations and citizens in all circumstances and at all levels of society

[126] The basis is the arrangements and legislation in normal conditions

[127] The vital functions of society are critical for the functioning of society and are subject to various threats

[128] These threats may stem from human actions, technological developments, or natural causes

[129] They are prevented and addressed through strategic tasks defined for different actors

[130] The strategic tasks are sets of duties based on legislation, formalized through agreements and complemented by voluntary activities

[131] Executing the tasks in all security situations requires both preparedness and response – in other words, measures to respond to threats

[132] Preparedness reduces the likelihood of threats realising and promotes society’s readiness to face threats

[133] Preparedness is based on response needs

[134] Response minimises the impacts of realised threats and promotes the recovery of the vital functions of society

[135] Response readiness stems from preparedness

[136] Individuals are key security actors in society

[137] They have a constitutional right to security but also a responsibility to act within their abilities in a manner that enhances security and the sense of security in communities and society

[138] The functional capacity of individuals and their knowledge, skills and security-strengthening attitude constitute the foundation of comprehensive security

[139] Mutual trust among people is a vital element in upholding society

[140] The concept for comprehensive security in brief

[141] Key concepts Comprehensive security = A concept in which the vital functions of society are taken care of in collaboration between the authorities, business community, organisations and citizens

[142] Vital function of society = Functions that are essential for a functioning society

[143] Threat = A potential event or series of events which, when realised, may cause disruptions or crises that endanger the vital functions of society

[144] Strategic task = A task defined to safeguard the vital functions of society

[145] Preparedness = An activity that reduces the likelihood of threats realising, promotes readiness to face threats and creates the prerequisites for response

[146] Response = An activity used to respond to a threat, minimise the impact of realised threats and promote the recovery of the vital functions of society

[359] Disruptions and crises Realised threats cause disruptions and crises

[360] Disruptions are events or situations which do not cause long-term or significant harmful impacts on society’s operating conditions

[361] Crises are abnormal or exceptional events or situations that threaten the functions or existence of society as a whole

[362] Disruptions may require extensive resources but rarely threaten society as a whole

[363] Their impacts are usually short-term and local, affecting only certain functions

[364] Disruptions can usually be anticipated, although their timing, type and impact cannot be accurately predicted

[365] They can often be resolved by adhering to pre-prepared plans

[366] Disruptions may include situations that have a short-term or limited impact on the vital functions of society, such as interruptions in water supply, temporary interruptions in payment transactions, or brief power outages

[367] Crises may arise independently or escalate from a disruption that has been unsuccessfully controlled

[368] They may be actively aggravated either from within society or through external hostile influencing

[369] Crises call for strategic, adaptable and timely responses to ensure that society’s operating conditions are preserved

[370] Crises are typically one-off and rare events or situations which often extend across organisational, geographical and sectoral borders

[371] Some crises can be anticipated, but the moment of their occurrence and their impact are often difficult to estimate

[372] The extent and duration of the impacts of crises may be considerable

[373] They can rarely be resolved by means of predefined operating methods and plans alone but require proactivity and flexibility

[374] Examples of crises include the use of military force and a pandemic

[420] Preparedness The goal of preparedness is to reduce the likelihood of threats realising, promote readiness to face threats and create the prerequisites for response

[421] It builds and maintains the capability needed for implementing a response

[422] Preparedness is based on the goals and objectives defined in connection with planning, the predefined operating methods and the identified resource requirements

[423] Preparedness is based on the preparedness obligation laid down in the Emergency Powers Act, Rescue Act and other legislation, agreements and voluntary action

[424] It is managed, supervised and coordinated by each ministry in their respective sectors

[425] Preparedness extends to all levels of operations and encompasses the coordination of the activities of the business community, organisations and other communities with the public sector measures

[426] It often also requires cross-border measures

[427] Preparedness by the authorities and other actors in public administration alone is insufficient to safeguard the vital functions of society

[428] For preparedness to be effective, the participation of society as a whole is required

[429] Everyone is also responsible, according to their abilities, for their own preparedness

[430] An essential aspect of preparedness is the acquisition and maintenance of the resources required by the task

[431] At least the following areas need to be considered in preparedness: * the operating methods that steer the use of resources to achieve the goals * the organisation in which the resources are organised as required by the task * training that develops the competence required by the task * the material with which actors are equipped * leadership to steer the activities to achieve objectives * sufficient personnel with appropriate competence * infrastructure that supports the implementation of the task * information needed for the activities

[432] Preparedness is implemented by coordinating operating methods, organisation, training, material, leadership, personnel, infrastructure, and information through collective planning and joint exercises

[433] The goal is to produce readiness and to demonstrate the capacity to execute it

[434] Good practices include the National Defence Courses, preparedness exercises led by the public administration and the exercises by the National Emergency Supply Organisation

[435] Exercises bring together different areas of capability and hone their collaboration to reach the collective goals

[436] This also increases mutual trust and credibility by demonstrating the capacity to carry out the planned task

[437] Joint exercises also help identify the links and interfaces between activities, areas in need of development in the preparedness plans, and shortcomings in the resources required for their implementation

[438] Joint exercises are at the core of preparedness

[439] Response The goal of response is to minimise the impact of realised threats, promote the recovery of the vital functions of society and provide grounds for preparedness

[440] Response applies the capability built and maintained through preparedness to achieve the desired impact

[441] Response is based on the duties and normal powers of authorities and other organisations with statutory preparedness responsibilities

[442] These may be expanded in separately identified emergency conditions which cannot be controlled through the regular powers

[443] As a rule, response is carried out in accordance with the normal operating procedures and organisation in normal conditions, following the principle of the competent authority

[444] Society’s other resources can also be allocated to support the competent authority

[445] Attention is paid to securing the basic services of society when considering such allocation

[446] Contingency plans that provide grounds for preparedness are drawn up to implement response

[447] Preparing for response involves regulating the availability of resources, for example through different levels of readiness

[448] There must be capacity to regulate readiness flexibly

[449] At the highest level of readiness, all resources in accordance with the contingency plan are deployed

[450] When necessary, additional resources are obtained as part of the preparation and their use is prioritised in advance

[451] The goal is to ensure that the resources and powers required by the situation, threat and need are available in a timely manner

[452] Sufficiently accurate and up-to-date situational awareness and timely decisions are prerequisites for this

[453] The preparation must also consider the additional powers that may be deployed in disruptions and crises

[454] When necessary, it must be possible to enforce the additional powers granted pursuant to the Emergency Powers Act, which strengthen the authorities’ capacity to carry out their duties

[455] Society may experience several disruptions and crises simultaneously, and they may also extend beyond state borders

[456] Such cases highlight the capacity to cooperate, exchange information, act flexibly and make agile changes in resource allocation

[457] Disruptions and crises may also become long-lasting

[458] In such cases, particular emphasis is placed on actions related to individuals’ well-being, who must be capable of balancing the workload and enabling recovery from the strain caused by operational readiness

[459] Due to the diversity of threats and the complexity of situations, it is not always possible to prepare for each situation separately

[460] The emphasis is therefore on general resilience built through long-term preparedness

[461] This resilience enables the ability to cope with unexpected situations and act flexibly and effectively even in unforeseen circumstances

[462] Once a threat realises, capability built through preparedness is used to reach the objectives set for the operations

[463] The measures countering the threat aim to minimise its impacts, much like the measures taken to contain a forest fire

[464] As a rule, the goal is to eliminate the threat

[465] The objectives of measures can include restricting, directing, delaying or mitigating the threat

[466] When containing the situation, active and proactive measures are taken to seize the initiative

[467] When resolving the situation, a maximised impact is usually pursued by means of focused operations

[468] Response also includes measures to protect one’s own operations by decentralising and multiplying critical operations and by, for example, warning the population

[469] The objective of these measures is to ensure continuous and disruption-free operations

[470] The objectives of the measures may include protecting the operations or promoting recovery

[471] As a rule, the objective is to ensure the continuity of a vital function

[472] The use of response can also restore resources to use in potential further measures

[473] At the same time, resources not engaged in the operations are allocated to promote recovery as much as possible

[474] In Finland, the implementation of response measures is regulated by the competent authorities and decision-makers

[475] In situations that extensively challenge society, such as hybrid influencing, coordination of activities and collaboration between actors is crucial

[476] In NATO, the implementation of response is guided by the NATO response system, which, as the system that defines the Alliance’s level of readiness and response, is intended to ensure readiness and support for comprehensive decision-making in all types of crisis situations

[477] Increased interdependencies also call for increased capacity to use EU-level, Nordic, and global structures

[518] General principles The general principles of safeguarding the vital functions of society are democracy, rule of law, a focus on the individual, collaboration, inclusion, competence and openness

[520] Democracy entails the right of the individual to participate in and influence the development of society and their living conditions

[521] Rule of law means that the exercise of public powers must be based on law, and in all public activity, the law must be strictly observed

[522] As a country based on the rule of law, Finland also respects and secures the basic and human rights and promotes equality and non-discrimination

[523] Focus on the individual highlights the role of citizens as key actors in our comprehensive security

[524] Everyone has the right to security, but individuals must also provide it according to their abilities, both for themselves and for others as members of communities and society

[525] At individual level, it is important to maintain adequate physical, psychological, social and ethical functional capacity, and to identify, anticipate and respond decisively to the factors that threaten it

[526] Ultimately, it is a question of a security attitude and a will to maintain and develop security

[527] Collaboration is emphasised, as interdependencies in our society are constantly growing

[528] The use of resources in different situations must be optimised in a manner that best serves the overall situation

[529] This calls for the capacity to recognise the purpose of one’s own actions as part of a larger whole, the readiness to bear responsibility that is greater than oneself, the willingness to help, and altruism to be ready to give up something personal for the common good if necessary

[530] Mutual trust based on transparency, predictability and commitment is the cornerstone of collaboration

[531] Inclusion is necessary for the efficient and appropriate use of limited resources in safeguarding vital functions

[532] This requires that collaboration between the public sector, business community, organisations and networks has been secured in all sectors and levels of operation

[533] Adequate resources must be secured for preparedness and response

[534] Cost-effectiveness is promoted by developing and coordinating procedures and systems in normal conditions so that they can also be applied during disruptions and emergency conditions

[535] Competence emphasises the statutory obligation and right to participate in security operations

[537] Strategic tasks are based on legislation and the responsibility of the competent authority

[538] Other actors support the competent authority and maintain the capacity to carry out their primary duty in an appropriate manner

[539] Openness increases and harmonises individuals’ security awareness, promotes commitment to security measures and strengthens psychological resilience

[540] It also contributes to preventing threats

[541] Information related to preparedness and security should be as open as possible, although for security reasons, not all information can be extensively shared

[2085] The prerequisites for the population’s recovery are developed in advance because social well-being is a strength that also helps face disruptions

[2086] The population’s recovery is promoted through aftercare, which begins while the disruption or crisis is still in progress

[2087] Situational awareness helps assess the long-term effects of disruptions on society and individuals

[2088] Activities supporting recovery reduce the subsequent vulnerability of individuals and communities and aim to prevent new crises

[2089] Consideration is given in the recovery plans to the impacts of equality and non-discrimination on minorities and vulnerable population groups

[2090] Depending on the disruption, the recovery process is monitored and supported, both in the immediate community and at national level

[2091] The aim of aftercare is to develop and support the resilience of individuals and society

[2092] Concept of operation** The support need of the population is identified to enable recovery

[2093] In Finland, information suitable for this purpose is collected regularly through School Health Promotion studies and periodic citizen surveys

[2094] Occupational health services also gather information about the population’s need for support

[2095] Contacts by the services of different organisations can also indicate needs, and information can be gathered by customised collection of information carried out by wellbeing services counties and municipalities, for example

[2096] A situational awareness analysis requires better coordination at local and regional levels

[2097] Good planning and communication may be decisive in successful recovery

[2098] Confrontation can be prevented through communications and inclusive and experimental new operating methods

[2099] The actors in the recovery stage are not necessarily the same as those in the initial stage of the situation

[2100] In addition to communication, strengthening the sense of inclusion in people is also required

[2101] Through their operations, the authorities must create opportunities for exchanging experiences, doing things together, rebuilding and restoring trust

[2102] The required aftercare is initiated, and its implementation is monitored

[2103] The planning and implementation of aftercare is supported by means of research

[2104] Recovering from disruptions may take a long time, and this requires the assessment of the need for separate financing

[2105] Systematic communication is key in aftercare

[2106] Open and accessible communication helps calm the situation, restore the sense of security and strengthen the development of psychological resilience

[2107] An important role in recovery is played by the region’s social welfare and healthcare, as well as cooperation between multiple authorities and the early childhood education and care units, educational institutions, and organisations

[2108] Community support considers families, next of kin, sporting associations and other organisations, which are also active providers of support

[2109] Extensive use of the voluntary activities provided by organisations in the aftercare process speeds up the recovery of communities and individuals and strengthens resilience

[2110] Actors** The actors essential for recovery include municipalities (libraries, schools, daycare), wellbeing services counties (wellbeing, healthcare, social services, security), the business community, workplaces, religious communities, organisations, associations and cultural services

[2111] Organisations can assist in establishing situational awareness of the population’s resilience

[2112] The activities of organisations and volunteers strengthen the skills of municipalities, communities and individuals as they recover from the post-crisis situation
