[1] B. NORWEGIAN SECURITY AND DEFENCE POLICY

[2] Security Policy Objectives There is a broad consensus about the fundamental objectives of Norwegian security policy

[3] These objectives are listed in the long-term plan (St.prp. nr. 45 (2000-2001) Restructuring Norway’s Defence Establishment

[4] The Armed Forces will contribute to: prevent and counter challenges to Norwegian security action in support of Norwegian rights, interests and obligations the conduct of other tasks of importance to society in selected areas securing facilities and activities that are vital to Norwegian society

[5] Main Tasks of the Military Defence Norwegian defence policy is based on four mutually reinforcing principles

[6] A balanced and flexible national defence * Military co-operation with allies and participation in international defence-related co-operation * Total defence concept and other civil and military defence-related co-operation

[7] The principle of conscription Defence policy forms the central plank of the Government’s security policy and the Norwegian Armed Forces represent our principal instrument of this policy

[8] The tasks of the Defence Establishment can be grouped under eight main headings

[9] To maintain a military presence in priority areas • Intelligence gathering and the surveillance of Norwegian areas of interest

[10] Upholding national sovereignty and the exercise of national authority

[11] Crisis management in areas under Norwegian control

[12] The defence of Norwegian territory and preparing, together with allies, to meet any challenge to Norwegian security

[13] Securing facilities and activities that are vital to Norwegian society Involvement in multinational operations

[14] Assisting civil society. These functions provide guidelines for the development of Norway’s Armed Forces

[15] The Total Defence Concept Civil-military co-operation is a continuation and a further development of the Total Defence concept

[16] Although civil-military co-operation also incorporates new elements, the principles of the Total Defence concept remain valid

[17] The further development of the Total Defence concept should be seen in the light of new challenges related to, inter alia, the increased vulnerability of a modern society, additional security challenges and changes with regard to Armed Forces’ need for civil support

[18] Civil-military co-operation includes three elements: Civil support to the Armed Forces, Armed Forces’ support to civil society, and management at governmental level

[19] Civil support to the Armed Forces includes, inter alia, the provision of support in situations in which contingency legislation comes into force, i.e. in an Article 5 situation

[20] Consequently, the Armed Forces may, in situations calling for a high state of alertness, call on civilian resources to ensure required support subject to the identified needs of the Armed Forces

[21] Thus, such support may be provided across a variety of areas

[22] In addition, civil support comprises supportive measures if and when contingency legislation does not come into force

[23] To the extent that such legislation is not applied, acquisition of appropriate resources will be ensured through other means than requisition

[24] This is likely during low intensity crisis and if Norway contributes militarily to crisis response operations

[25] Furthermore, civil-military co-operation includes military support to civil society

[26] It is a firmly established principle that the Armed Forces shall support civil society to the extent this is consistent with the Armed Forces’ military tasks and available resources

[27] In addition, civil-military co-operation includes management at governmental level

[28] Several different institutions are tasked to protect and ensure co-operation between civil and military authorities

[29] Authority in Peacetime and during War Under the Norwegian Constitution, the highest executive power is vested in His Majesty the King

[30] Also according to the Constitution, the King is the Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces

[31] This authority is exercised in the Council of State, i.e. by the Government

[32] The Government makes decisions in special Government Conferences not attended by the King

[33] Decisions on important matters are afterwards formally adopted by the King in Council

[34] Matters concerning mobilisation and defence plans are decided outside the Council

[35] The Government is the highest executive authority responsible for military and civil preparedness in peacetime and for the command of all aspects of national defence in time of war

[36] The military command authority is delegated to military commanders

[37] The Minister of Defence heads the Royal Norwegian Ministry of Defence

[38] He carries the constitutional and political responsibility for the activities of the Armed Forces

[39] All matters not decided in Council are decided by him or on his behalf by the Ministry of Defence

[40] The Minister of Defence presents such matters to the King in the presence of the Prime Minister and the Minister of Foreign Affairs alone

[41] The responsibility for civil emergency planning lies with the Directorate of Civil Defence and Emergency Planning, which falls under the Royal Norwegian Ministry of Justice

[42] All other ministries are responsible for emergency planning in their own sectors

[43] The Chief of Defence is the country’s highest ranking military official, and is principal military adviser to the Government and the Minister of Defence on military matters

[44] He has general command of Norwegian Defence in peacetime, with the exception of certain administrative limitations

[45] In time of war, the Chief of Defence continues to be the Government’s closest adviser, while the command authority is assumed to be transferred to NATO’s Integrated Command Structure

[52] Security Policy Fundamentals Background Norway’s strategic outlook is primarily shaped by the country’s position within a power structure where the dominant actors are NATO, the USA, the EU and Russia

[53] The relative relationship between these actors has changed considerably over the last decade

[54] Russia’s position has been weakened, while the USA and the EU have increased their relative influence and importance in the world

[55] The role and tasks of NATO have over the same period of time been subject to change

[56] The growing momentum behind forging a common European Security and Defence Policy is one important development that influences Norwegian policy considerations, together with the ongoing restructuring of NATO

[57] The alliance has been expanded, with three new members, and its strategic concept has been adapted to meet a wider array of tasks than during the cold war

[58] This is a process that will put heavy demands on the military forces of the alliance

[59] Strong emphasis is put on the ability of the individual members to be able to operate effectively together with other allies and partner-countries

[60] This demand is reflected in new requirements regarding training, equipment, materiel, organisation and qualifications of the individual soldier or officer

[61] Norwegian territory remains a strategically important part of the Euro-Atlantic area, a position that is reinforced also due to the natural resources we manage

[62] Norwegian oil and gas reserves are of considerable strategic importance

[63] Future Security Challenges The potential risks facing us in the future will be quite different than the ones we faced during the cold war

[64] The challenges and risks inherent in the new strategic picture are much more unpredictable and are characterised by a blurring of the distinction between what constitutes national and international security

[65] An increasing level of interdependence dominates the relationship between states in all parts of the world

[66] Threats may arise and develop fast and without much warning, not least because non-state actors can pose a threat to security

[67] All these factors combined constitute new challenges that will have consequences for the Norwegian armed forces in their role as a tool for ensuring security policy objectives

[68] The Russia of today does not pose a military threat to Norway

[69] The relationship between Norway and Russia will, however, for the foreseeable future be characterised by the inherent asymmetry that exists between a small country and a major power

[70] Managing this relationship will be a challenge to Norwegian security policy

[71] New challenges have arisen, and some of these may develop into potential threats

[72] Many of these may be described as threats of an asymmetric character, i.e. threats that are directed towards the weak spots of a modern open society that to a large extent is dependent on technology

[73] Terrorism, whether performed by state or non-state actors, or a combination of these, poses a growing threat to international security and stability

[74] Society, with its strong dependence on electronics and information technology has become far more vulnerable than before

[75] The advent of cyber-terrorism creates new challenges regarding intelligence and protection

[76] The spread of weapons of mass destruction may come to represent a growing threat to the international community in the years to come, as also non-state actors may attempt to make use of such weapons for purposes of terror

[77] The last decade has shown how ethnic and religious divides may result in violent conflict

[78] It is expected that conflicts of this character will constitute a security challenge for Europe for the years to come

[79] A broad approach is necessary in order to manage these evolving security challenges

[80] Military force in this context represents one of several tools that are available as means to reach policy goals

[81] This requires a broadly based approach to security and underlines the importance of coordination of military force with political, economic and diplomatic policies

[82] The probability for Norway of being drawn into lower scale crises may be on the rise

[83] There is also an increased likelihood that several minor conflicts may occur at the same time

[84] Contentions between states or groups may spread in new and unexpected directions and cause new conflicts

[85] Norway may indirectly become involved in conflicts

[86] The potential threats facing us as a part of a more globalized world have thus not disappeared; they are indeed more numerous and complex than before

[87] The CFE Treaty (Conventional Forces in Europe

[88] Since the signing of the CFE Treaty, over 58,000 battle tanks, armoured combat vehicles, artillery pieces, combat aircraft and attack helicopters have been destroyed

[89] The signatory countries’ stocks of such weapons have been cut not only down to, but actually below, the levels agreed

[90] Negotiations on adapting the CFE Treaty to the new political and security patterns in Europe have been in progress since

[91] The Final Act on the adopted Treaty was signed at the OSCE Summit in Istanbul in November

[92] The so-called flank regime is retained as a legally binding part of the adopted Treaty, a fact that had an important bearing on Norway’s acceptance of the Treaty

[93] Retention of the flank regime means that constraints are still imposed on Russia’s flexibility with regard to force concentrations and dispositions in the flank area

[94] This in turn contributes towards increased predictability in Norway’s neighbouring areas, an important factor in our defence planning

[95] The majority of the signatories to the adopted Treaty, however, have not yet commenced the formal ratification process

[98] Thus in many countries the process still has a considerable way to go before the CFE Treaty can take effect

[100] NATO restructuring NATO has played a decisive role in ensuring Norway’s security throughout the post-war years and it will continue as the main foundation of our defence in the future

[101] In recent years the NATO Alliance has undergone a process of almost continuous change both internally and with regard to its relations with other countries and organisations

[102] Its collective defence capability has been strengthened through reforms in its force structure, command structure and integrated military planning

[103] Relations with its European neighbours have become closer through the establishment and development of the Partnership for Peace (PfP), The Euro-Atlantic Partnership Council (EAPC) and a broader, more formalised framework of cooperation with Russia and Ukraine

[104] Poland, the Czech Republic and Hungary became members of the Alliance on 12 March

[105] At the Washington Summit in April that year, the New Strategic Concept was formally adopted

[106] It was also agreed that the Defence Capabilities Initiative should be developed further

[107] The Summit also saw the launch of a separate programme of further assistance to those countries aspiring to future membership of the Alliance, the so-called Membership Action Plan

[108] The comprehensive changes that NATO has undergone in recent years have consolidated NATO’s position as the central player in the field of European security and the core of the European security structure

[109] These changes have, however, to some extent blurred the distinction between the political and military aspects of NATO cooperation

[110] The process of change is not yet complete and must be regarded as a continuous process of evolution to adapt the Alliance to the new challenges that are constantly arising such as, for example, those experienced in the aftermath of the Kosovo crisis

[111] The topic of increased European responsibility in matters of security and defence has been the subject of discussion in NATO, WEU and EU forums for some years

[112] Since the autumn of 1998, however, there has been a marked speeding up of this development

[113] A preliminary structure for decision making on questions of defence and security policy has already been established between EU member states, including a Political and Security Committee (PSC), EU Military Committee and an EU Military Staff

[114] In addition, third country arrangements are established both on PSC level and the military levels

[115] During the course of 2001 the existing defence-related functions of the WEU will be transferred to the EU

[116] The legal basis of the WEU, the Treaty of Brussels dating from 1948, will however remain in place

[117] From the Norwegian side there is support for the establishment of a Common European Security and Defence Policy

[118] At the same time Norway would emphasise the importance of ensuring that this process within the EU should not be allowed to weaken collaboration within NATO and that the work of developing a CESDP structure is coordinated with NATO

[119] Within the framework of collaboration between the EU members and third party states, Norway will seek to make an active contribution both in connection with the EU-led crisis management structure now under development and in the context of discussing security and defence matters of a more general nature

[120] The EU’s aim is that member states, in response to international crises where NATO as a whole is not engaged, should be capable of deploying a rapid reaction force numbering up to 60,000 with the necessary support elements

[121] The force will consist of national and multinational components

[122] Among other contributions, the Nordic brigade structure may form a specific contribution to the EU’s crisis management structure

[123] Norway’s military contribution to the CESDP may well form part of a Nordic component in this context

[124] The EU stresses that there is no question of setting up a “European Army”

[125] It is also clear that the eventual rapid reaction force must be capable of the full range of so-called Petersberg tasks (i.e. humanitarian, rescue, peacekeeping and crisis management including peacemaking) but not “Article 5” tasks (i.e. collective self-defence). Allied reinforcement plans in the event of crisis or war form a central pillar of Norwegian security and defence policy

[142] With regard to NATO’s strategy, the situation in the world today means that it makes less sense to link reinforcement planning to pre-defined scenarios simply because it is extremely difficult to foresee what situations may arise

[143] NATO has therefore moved away from a system of pre-prepared reinforcement options and the selection of particular “customised” plans, and more towards flexible and mobile forces with a variety of insertion options
